Two Coherent Systems

Marketing picks how many models to offer; manufacturing picks the batch size and the equipment. Each choice raises or lowers the value of the other, so the pairs that fit form coherent systems. Ford's and Toyota's are two of them. Move the environment and watch each system shift smoothly while the best one jumps.

Demand

How much customers value a wider product line. Raises the payoff of every extra model.

Total output per year, in thousands of units. Scale favours dedicated, specialized equipment.

Technology

Cost of carrying one unit in stock for a year. Makes big batches and a wide line expensive together.

Cost of one changeover on flexible equipment (Toyota's quick die changes). Specialized equipment stays at 50.

Extra cost per unit with flexible equipment: the scale economies it gives up.

The answer

The model

π(n, b, θ) = m·Q + A·ln n − Sθ·Q / b − h·n·b / 2 − Δcθ·Q,   n ≥ 1,  b > 0,  θ ∈ {specialized: S = 50, Δc = 0;  flexible: S = SF, Δc}
Within one equipment type: b* = S·Q / A,  n* = 2A² / (h·S·Q),  π* = m·Q + A·ln n* − 2A − Δc·Q;  corner n* = 1 when 2A² ≤ h·S·Q, then b* = √(2S·Q / h)
Manufacturing switches to flexible equipment above the switch point ns = (Δc·Q)² / (2Q·h·(√50 − √SF)²).  A system is coherent when its own n* lies on its own side of ns.

The two-manager set-up, the decreasing best replies, Ford's point (low variety, large batches) and the niche/modern point (many varieties, small batches) are Milgrom & Roberts (1992), Ch. 4 "Complementarities, Innovation Attributes, and Coordination Failure", pp. 111–113, Figure 4.3; the equipment contrast is Ch. 1, pp. 4–5 (Toyota) and Ch. 4, p. 113. M&R draw the figure without a formula. The functional form here (log taste for variety, setup cost per changeover, average inventory b/2 per model) is this page's own and fixed: specialized changeover cost 50, margin m = 2. All numbers are illustrative calibration, not data.

Reading M&R's footnote 9 carefully. M&R describe the profit surface over Figure 4.3 as "a mountain with three peaks". With two smooth decreasing best replies crossing three times, the middle crossing is a saddle point, not a peak. Here the equipment switch makes the batch curve jump, and two peaks remain. The book's point stands: there is more than one coherent system, and only one of them is best.
