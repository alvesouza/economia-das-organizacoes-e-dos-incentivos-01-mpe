The Mixing Square

Any 2×2 game, reduced to the four numbers that decide every best response: how much each player gains from their first strategy over their second, against each of the rival’s strategies. Move them and watch the two best-response correspondences in the (q, p) square re-form; every crossing is a Nash equilibrium, pure or mixed. The two belief sliders show the indifference condition at work: a player mixes only when the rival’s mix makes the two pure strategies pay the same.

Tadelis (2013) §6.2.1–6.2.3, printed pp. 108–114 (default = the game of §6.2.3, p. 113; Fig. 6.4, p. 114); Prop. 6.1, p. 108; Gibbons (1992) §1.3.B, pp. 40–44, Figs. 1.3.8–1.3.12

    Why four numbers are enough: adding a constant to player 1’s payoffs in one column (or to player 2’s in one row) changes no best response, so the page plays the normalized game in the matrix above, whose best responses and equilibria are exactly those of the original. Expressions: player 1’s gain from row 1 at belief q is qα1 + (1−q)β1; player 2’s gain from column 1 at belief p is pα2 + (1−p)β2; a player best-responds with the first strategy when the gain is positive, the second when negative, and any mix when it is zero (Prop. 6.1, p. 108). Equilibria are found exactly, piece by piece of the square, with no grid search. Defaults reproduce Tadelis §6.2.3: (M, R), (D, C) and the mixed equilibrium p = 1/6, q = 3/7. Presets: Matching Pennies, p. 108 (α1=2, β1=−2, α2=−2, β2=2); Battle of the Sexes, p. 69 (2, −1, 1, −2: mixed at p = 2/3, q = 1/3); Stag Hunt, p. 84 (2, −3, 2, −3: mixed at 3/5); Prisoner’s Dilemma in the Lecture 1 payoffs, Tadelis p. 48 (−1 throughout: (F, F) only). Notes: Lecture 2 notes L02-07 (mixed strategies) and L02-09 (existence).
