Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Lecture 5, "Repeated games I: cooperation": finite repetition, the carrot and the stick, and the objects of the infinite game. The lecture has no slides yet, so build it from `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis §10.1–10.2, pp. 190–197, with §9.4–9.5 for the multistage background), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.3.A and the start of §2.3.B, pp. 82–97) and the Cabral `Introduction to Industrial Organization` source (§7.3, pp. 172–174). The listener is an economics master's student who knows Nash equilibrium, backward induction and subgame perfection from Lectures 2–4. Keep every claim precise, and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

The question: one-shot prisoner's dilemma play is fink-fink, by dominance. Can repetition change that? Frame the whole episode around one idea: the future can discipline today only if tomorrow can be made to depend on today.

A unique stage equilibrium unravels: state Tadelis Def. 10.1 of G(T, delta) and Prop. 10.1, that a unique stage Nash equilibrium gives a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, the stage equilibrium in every period after every history. Prove it aloud by induction from the end. In the last period no future depends on the action, so play is the stage equilibrium. One period earlier the continuation adds the same constant to every cell, and a constant cannot change a best response, so the game is strategically the stage game again. Use Gibbons's Figure 2.3.2, where folding back adds (1,1) to every cell. Stress that neither the horizon (2 or 500 periods) nor the size of delta enters the proof; what matters is a known last period and a unique stage equilibrium.

Two stage equilibria give a carrot and a stick: walk through Tadelis's three-by-three example on p. 191, the prisoner's dilemma plus a third action pair R, r worth (3,3). Find its two pure equilibria and say why they are Pareto ranked. Present the strategies: play (M,m) in stage 1, then (R,r) if (M,m) was played and (F,f) otherwise. Derive footnote 2: cooperating gives 4 plus 3 delta, the best deviation 5 plus delta, so the profile is an SPE iff delta is at least 1/2. Name the trade-off: a gain of 1 today against a loss of 2 tomorrow. Count the strategy's instructions, one first-stage action and nine contingent ones, to show why a strategy covers histories that never happen. Then Gibbons's Figures 2.3.3–2.3.4: the three subgame-perfect outcomes, and why the one whose first stage is not a stage equilibrium is qualitatively new. Connect it to the Prisoner-Revenge Game of Tadelis §9.1: non-Nash play early needs multiple continuation equilibria later plus enough patience.

Renegotiation: discuss Gibbons pp. 86–88. The stick hurts the punisher too, so after a deviation both prefer (3,3), and "bygones are bygones" empties the threat. Explain Gibbons's fix with Figure 2.3.5: punishments that sit on the Pareto frontier of the stage equilibria, so the punisher has nothing to renegotiate toward.

Discounting and its readings: define the present value (Tadelis Def. 10.2) and show why delta below 1 is needed, since two infinite sums cannot be ranked. Then the key reinterpretation: delta as the probability that the relationship continues, which gives the same expected sum. At delta = 0.97 the chance of reaching 500 periods is below one in four million, yet in every period a future is still ahead, so there is never a last period to unravel from. Mention the product of continuation probability and time discounting (footnote 4) and Gibbons's (1 minus p) over (1 plus r). Use Cabral's "indefinitely repeated game" as the applied reading.

The average payoff: derive the finite sum s(v, delta, T) aloud by multiplying by delta and subtracting, take T to infinity to get v over (1 minus delta), and explain why multiplying by (1 minus delta) (Def. 10.3) puts a constant stream v back at v. Say why that normalization lets repeated-game payoffs be drawn on the same axes as stage payoffs, which Part 2 needs for the folk theorem.

Histories and strategies: explain Def. 10.4, a strategy as a map from every history to an action, and count the 64 information sets at stage 4 of the repeated prisoner's dilemma. Add Gibbons's two subtle points: every subgame of the infinite game is identical to the whole game, and the stage-t game alone is not a subgame, because treating it as one assumes it is the last. Close Part 1 with Prop. 10.2: playing a stage Nash equilibrium after every history is an SPE for any delta, so the stick is always available for free.
