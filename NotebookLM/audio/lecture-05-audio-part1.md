The listener is an economics master's student who has heard Lectures 3 and 4. Keep every claim precise, and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

The stage game: Tadelis's prisoner's dilemma, mum or fink, with (M, m) paying (4, 4), (F, f) paying (1, 1), and the temptation and sucker payoffs 5 and minus 1. Label them T, R, P, S = 5, 4, 1, minus 1.

Finite repetition and unraveling: state Tadelis Prop. 10.1, if the stage game has a unique Nash equilibrium, the finitely repeated game has a unique subgame-perfect equilibrium, that equilibrium every period, whatever T and delta. Prove it aloud by backward induction: the last period is a one-shot game, so it must play the stage equilibrium (Tadelis Prop. 9.2); that play does not depend on the history, so the second-to-last period is a one-shot game too, and so on. A known last period unravels cooperation, whether the game lasts two periods or five hundred.

Carrots and sticks: Tadelis's two-stage game adds a third action to each player, with (R, r) paying (3, 3) and every other new cell zero, so the stage game has two equilibria, (F, f) and (R, r). Consider the strategy: play (M, m) first; if it was played, reward with (R, r), otherwise punish with (F, f). Derive the condition: cooperating gives 4 plus 3 delta, deviating 5 plus delta, so delta must be at least one half. Explain Tadelis's two requirements, two distinct stage equilibria, a carrot and a stick, and enough patience. Then Gibbons's renegotiation critique: after a deviation, both players prefer to move to the carrot, so a Pareto-dominated stick may not be believed.

Infinite repetition: define the present value, the sum of delta to the t minus 1 times the payoff, with delta below 1 so the sum converges. Explain delta as a continuation probability: with a chance of ending each period the effective factor is the survival probability times the time discount, and Tadelis's 0.97 to the 500th power, about 2.4 times 10 to the minus 7, shows how a long but uncertain horizon still discounts its tail. Define the average payoff, (1 minus delta) times the present value, and show that a constant stream averages to its constant. State Prop. 10.2: playing a stage equilibrium every period, regardless of history, is a subgame-perfect equilibrium for any delta.

Grim trigger: play M as long as every past period was (M, m), otherwise F forever. Compare cooperating forever, 4 over (1 minus delta), with finking today and being punished forever, 5 plus delta times 1 over (1 minus delta): cooperation holds iff delta is at least one quarter. Generalize to delta star = (T minus R) over (T minus P), a one-time gain against a per-period loss that lasts forever, and to Tadelis (10.5) for any stage game. Sign the comparative statics: the threshold rises with T and P, falls with R, and does not depend on S. Explain why S drops out, and read the threshold as an interest rate: with delta = 1 over (1 plus r), cooperation survives up to r of 300 percent. Say explicitly that why comparing only this single deviation suffices, and the check of the punishment phase, belong to Part 2.

The folk theorem: define a convex combination with Tadelis's example, 0.6 times (4, 4) plus 0.4 times (5, minus 1) gives (4.4, 2), and the feasible set as the convex hull of the stage payoffs. State Theorem 10.1: any feasible payoff vector giving every player more than a stage Nash payoff can be approached by the average payoffs of a subgame-perfect equilibrium when delta is close enough to 1. Describe the region for this game, with corners (1, 1), (1, 4.6), (4, 4) and (4.6, 1), and derive the 4.6. Sketch Friedman's proof as in Gibbons: Nash reversion, with a threshold for each player below 1. Name, without proof, Fudenberg and Maskin's minimax version. Close on the interpretation: the theorem shows cooperation is possible, not that it happens; "almost anything goes" is a selection problem.
