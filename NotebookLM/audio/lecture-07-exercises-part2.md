Talk through, in detail (2/3), the adverse-selection exercises of the Lecture 7 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 07.pdf` (items 4, 6 and 9), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 07 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture07 - Bayesian Games, Adverse Selection and Signaling.pdf` (frames 23–25 and the appendix, frames 56–58), the Tadelis book (§12.3) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§13.B–13.C). The listener has heard Part 1 on PBE and has the statements in hand.

Begin with the slides' used cars. Good cars are worth 8 to the seller and 10 to the buyer; lemons 2 and 4; half of each. A buyer pays at most the average, 7, which is below 8, so good cars leave, buyers anticipate it, and only lemons trade, at a price between 2 and 4. Name the one move every exercise below repeats: the buyer must value the good conditional on the set of sellers who are actually willing to sell at that price, not at the population average. Then state the general model of the appendix: if the average buyer value is below the good sellers' reservation value, only low quality trades.

Item 4, Tadelis 12.5 (Not All That Glitters). A prospector with gold x, uniform on 0 to 1, gets 3x by mining himself; the company would get 4x. At price p the prospector sells iff p is at least 3x, so the sellers are the types below p over 3. The company's expected value from those mines is 4 times the conditional mean, 4 times p over 6, that is 2p over 3, below p for every positive p; for p of 3 or more, everyone sells and the mean value 2 is below p. So the company rejects every positive price and no mine is sold, although trade would always be efficient. Each type gets 3x, on average 3/2, the company gets 0, and an expected surplus of 1/2 is lost. Traps: using the unconditional mean (at p = 2 the mines actually on offer are worth only 4/3 to the company, not 2); writing the threshold as p over 4; forgetting to rule out prices nobody asks; saying the prospector's ask is unique, when only the outcome is.

Item 6, Tadelis 12.6 (Reap and Weep). A farmer of talent theta, uniform on 0 to 1, earns theta by farming; at the plant he is worth 3/2 theta. A farmer works iff the wage w is at least theta, so the pool at w has mean talent w over 2 and is worth 3/4 w to the owner, below w. The owner rejects every positive wage, the market unravels and the surplus is 1/2. Part (c), the policy: cutting water subsidies halves the farming payoff to theta over 2. Now the pool at w is theta up to 2w, with mean w, worth 3/2 w, above w, so trade is profitable. In the best equilibrium everyone works at a wage up to 3/4 and the surplus is 3/4, the first best. Give the honest caveats from the solutions: other equilibria exist, in which the owner accepts wages only up to a lower cutoff, with surplus 1/4 plus 2 times that cutoff squared, worse than 1/2 when the cutoff is below root 2 over 4. And high-talent farmers lose. The lesson: a worse outside option for sellers weakens adverse selection. Traps: using the population mean in (a); conditioning on theta up to w instead of up to 2w in (c); comparing only the best equilibria; ignoring who loses.

Item 9, MWG 13.C.5 (regulated price and advertising). Part (a): a risk-neutral consumer buys at the regulated price p iff lambda v_H plus (1 minus lambda) v_L is at least p, so iff lambda is at least (p minus v_L) over (v_H minus v_L). Part (b): could advertising spending separate qualities? In a separating equilibrium the consumer buys only from the type that spends A_H. Because v_L is below p, she never buys from a firm known to be low quality. The high type is willing to spend at most p minus c_H. The low type would copy if A_H is below p minus c_L, which is larger because c_L is below c_H. So any A_H the high type can afford, the low type can afford too: no separating PBE. This is the reverse of the Spence condition on frame 45: separation needs the signal to be cheaper for the good type, and here, with one regulated price, the bad type gains more from a sale. Traps: "high quality advertises more because it can afford it"; forgetting that advertising is paid even without a sale; using the prior instead of Bayes' rule on the path; concluding that advertising never signals, when repeat purchases or a free price would change the answer.

Close by comparing Akerlof and Spence, as frame 50 does: one is the problem, private quality driving good products out; the other is one remedy, a costly action that works only if it costs the good type less.
