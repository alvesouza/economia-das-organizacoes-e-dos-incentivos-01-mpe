Talk through, in detail (3/3), the two hardest Bayesian-game exercises of the Lecture 6 real-exercise list, with continuous actions or continuous types: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 06.pdf` (items 8 and 9), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 06 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture06 - Bargaining and Bayesian Games.pdf` (frames 28–30 for the definitions, frame 37 for the homework) and the Tadelis (Ch. 12) and Gibbons (§3.1.A) books. The listener has heard Parts 1 and 2 and has the statements in hand. Reason aloud through every derivation.

Item 8, Tadelis 12.2 (Cournot Revisited), which is the slides' homework 1. Demand is a minus b times total output. Each firm's cost is c_L with probability mu and c_H otherwise, independently, and each firm knows only its own cost. First, what a strategy is here: a pair of quantities, one for the low-cost type and one for the high-cost type. Then the step that makes the problem tractable: a firm does not know the rival's output, but its profit is linear in that output, so only the rival's expected output, q-bar, matters. A type with cost c maximizes (a minus c minus b q-bar minus b q) times q. Its first-order condition gives q(c) = (a minus c minus b q-bar) over 2b, and the second-order condition holds because the objective is concave. Write the two conditions, one per type, and the definition of q-bar as mu q_L plus (1 minus mu) q_H. Average the two conditions to get q-bar = (a minus c-bar) over 3b, where c-bar is the mean cost, and substitute back. The low-cost type produces (a minus c_L) over 3b plus (1 minus mu)(c_H minus c_L) over 6b. The high-cost type produces (a minus c_H) over 3b minus mu(c_H minus c_L) over 6b. Interpret the corrections: compared with facing a rival known to have the same cost, a low-cost firm produces more, because the rival is on average less aggressive, and a high-cost firm produces less. Then do the slides' version, with profit q_i(theta_i minus q_i minus q_j): the first-order condition is q_i = (theta_i minus E[q_j]) over 2. Take expectations of both firms' conditions to solve for the expected outputs, then substitute: q_i equals theta_i over 2 minus (2E[theta_j] minus E[theta_i]) over 6. Traps: plugging in the rival's output for the rival's own realized type, as if you knew it; solving complete-information Cournot at the average cost and calling it the answer; writing one first-order condition per firm instead of one per type; assuming symmetry instead of showing the linear system has a unique solution; forgetting to check that outputs are positive.

Item 9, Tadelis 12.9 (Grab the Dollar). Part (a), complete information: a lone grabber gets 1, a player who does not grab gets 0, and both get minus 1 if both grab. The pure equilibria are the two in which exactly one player grabs. In the mixed equilibrium, grabbing gives 1 minus 2p against a rival who grabs with probability p, so indifference gives p equal to one half. Part (b): a lone grabber now gets 1 plus theta_i, with theta_i uniform on minus epsilon to epsilon and privately known. Look for a cutoff strategy, grab iff theta_i is at least theta-star. Facing a rival who grabs with probability q, grabbing gives (1 plus theta_i)(1 minus q) minus q, increasing in theta_i, which is why a cutoff is the right form. In the symmetric equilibrium the cutoff is zero: each player grabs with probability exactly one half, and the type at zero is indifferent, since 1 times one half equals one half. Mention the two asymmetric corner equilibria, one player always grabbing and the other never, which survive because the perturbation is small. Part (c): as epsilon goes to zero, each player's behavior looks like a coin flip with probability one half, exactly the mixed equilibrium of part (a). This is Harsanyi's purification: a mixed strategy can be read as pure behavior that depends on small private payoff differences the opponent cannot see. Nobody randomizes on purpose. Traps: using the wrong uniform probability (the density is 1 over 2 epsilon, so the probability above zero is one half for every epsilon); adding theta to every payoff instead of only to the lone-grab payoff; saying "the cutoff converges", when the point is that the grab probability matches the mixing probability; missing the corner equilibria.

Close with what links the two items: in every Bayesian game, write the strategy as a function of the player's own type, compute the expected payoff against the rival's strategy using the conditional distribution of the rival's type, and require a best response type by type. That is the slides' three-step recipe in continuous form.
