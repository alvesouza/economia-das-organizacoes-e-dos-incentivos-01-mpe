Talk through, in detail (1/3), the first exercises of the Lecture 4 real-exercise list: subgames, non-credible threats and entry. Solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 04.pdf` (items 1, 2, 3 and 6), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 04 - solutions.pdf` (the worked solutions and their "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture04 - Credible Threats and Subgame Perfection.pdf` and the books `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Ch. 7–8) and `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§2.3.B). The listener has the statements in hand.

Open with the slides' anchor, the Brexit game: nine pure Nash equilibria, and the one in which an unprepared UK "accepts only a favorable deal" rests on a threat it would never carry out (slide 8). A subgame starts at a single node and never cuts an information set (slides 14–16). A subgame-perfect equilibrium is Nash in every subgame, which removes non-credible threats (slide 17).

Item 1, Gibbons 2.12 (definitions in a repeated game). A strategy says what to play in every period after every possible history, including histories the plan itself never produces. In the two-stage prisoners' dilemma that means five instructions, so 32 pure strategies each. A subgame is what remains after a given history, so there is one for each history, and a single stage on its own is not a subgame. Subgame perfection requires Nash play after every history, on or off the path. The computed example: 16 Nash equilibria, all with defection in both stages, and exactly one SPE, defect after every history. Traps: a strategy written as a list of actions, one per period; calling the stage game played in period t a subgame; checking only the equilibrium path.

Item 2, Tadelis 7.6 (Entering an Industry). Build ordinal payoffs from the rankings: the outcome named neither best nor worst sits in the middle, which gives Out (1, 2), In and accommodate (2, 1), In and fight (0, 0). Each player has two pure strategies. The Nash equilibria are (In, A) and (Out, F), and with mixing, Out together with any plan that fights with probability at least one half. Only (In, A) is subgame perfect, because after entry accommodating gives 1 against 0. Traps: forgetting that the middle outcome is implied; answering only (In, A) when the question asks for Nash equilibria; saying (Out, F) is not Nash. The incumbent is indifferent on the path; the flaw lies off the path.

Item 3, Tadelis 8.8 (Entry Deterrence 1). Subtract the sunk entry cost of 2 after entry: Out gives (0, 10), war gives (minus 1, 1), accommodation gives (2, 4). The incumbent accommodates (4 against 1), so the entrant enters (2 against 0). The SPE is (In, A). The other Nash equilibria are Out with war probability at least two thirds, and they rest on a threat that costs nothing because it is never tested. The extension: a penalty b paid if it accommodates makes war credible when b exceeds 3, and such a commitment is worth up to 6 to the incumbent. This is the Brexit case's three lessons in miniature: talk is cheap, sunk investments can be strategic, and observability matters (slide 11). Traps: forgetting the sunk entry cost; calling (Out, W) an SPE because it is Nash; reporting only the outcome without the incumbent's plan.

Item 6, Tadelis 8.11 (Resident Assistant Selection). Player 1 ranks a, b, c; player 2 ranks b, a, c. Player 2's strategy is a triple, one veto after each of player 1's vetoes, so she has 8 strategies. Backward induction: after player 1 vetoes a she vetoes c, after b she vetoes c, after c she vetoes a. Player 1 therefore vetoes b, his rival's favorite, and a is chosen, with payoffs (2, 1). The SPE is unique. Of the 24 pure profiles, 7 are Nash and 6 of them are not subgame perfect. Three of those elect b through player 2's threat "if you veto b, I veto a", which she would not carry out, since a gives her 1 and c gives her 0. In part (d), player 2's e-mail removing a, her own second choice, gets her b: an irreversible move before player 1 acts works where the announced threat fails. Traps: player 1 vetoing his least preferred c, which player 2 would veto anyway; writing player 2's strategy as one veto; removing c or b in (d).

Close with the test that runs through all four: for each Nash equilibrium, go to every node off the path and ask whether the planned action is a best reply there.
