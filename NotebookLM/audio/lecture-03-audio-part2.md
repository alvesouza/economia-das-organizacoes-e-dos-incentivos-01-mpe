The sequential battle of the sexes: Alex finishes work at 3:00, goes to the opera or the football game and calls Chris at 4:45 to say "I am here"; Chris then chooses. Where should Chris go, and how do we represent the game?

The game tree: nodes with a precedence relation, a unique path between any two nodes, one predecessor per node, a root, terminal nodes carrying payoffs, and every other node assigned to a player or to Nature. Stress that the whole structure is common knowledge.

Information sets: state the three properties, and explain why every node in one set must have the same actions: otherwise the menu would reveal the node. Draw the simultaneous battle of the sexes as a tree with Chris's two nodes in one set: tree order is about information, not clock time. Define perfect information (singleton sets, no Nature) and imperfect information.

Strategies: a pure strategy maps every information set to an action, a complete plan including sets never reached. Work through the slides' Example 1: Chris has two strategies in the simultaneous game but four in the sequential one, OO, OF, FO and FF. Then mixed versus behavioral strategies: a mixed strategy draws one plan before play, a behavioral strategy mixes independently at each set. Count parameters for Chris: three for a mixed strategy, two for a behavioral one. State perfect recall and Kuhn's theorem (1953): with perfect recall, both generate the same outcome distributions, and say why forgetting breaks the equivalence.

Sequential rationality and backward induction: define sequential rationality, a best response at every information set. State the proposition: every finite game of perfect information has a sequentially rational backward-induction solution; and the corollary: a pure, sequentially rational Nash equilibrium exists, unique if no player is indifferent between two terminal nodes. Solve Example 2: Chris matches Alex, so Alex chooses opera, giving (O, OF). Then write the 2-by-4 normal form and find its three pure equilibria, (O, OO), (O, OF) and (F, FF). Explain that (F, FF) rests on Chris's non-credible threat to go to the football game even when Alex is at the opera, and that (O, OO) has the right outcome but an irrational plan off the path.

Subgames and subgame perfection: define a proper subgame, one node and all its successors, never cutting an information set. Define subgame-perfect equilibrium: the restriction to every proper subgame is a Nash equilibrium. Explain why every subgame-perfect equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium but not conversely, and state the Fact that in finite games of perfect information subgame-perfect equilibria are the backward-induction solutions. Mention the voluntary battle of the sexes, Example 3, only briefly; Lecture 4 works it out in full.

Explain the leader's gain as commitment, why total output rises, and why the game also has many Nash equilibria propped up by threats to flood the market, removed by subgame perfection.

The Cuban missile crisis, Example 5 (Tadelis §8.3.3): player 1 ignores, giving (0, 0), or escalates; player 2 then backs down, giving (10, minus 10), or proceeds to a simultaneous game where mutual retreat gives (minus 5, minus 5) and any Doomsday gives (minus 100, minus 100).
