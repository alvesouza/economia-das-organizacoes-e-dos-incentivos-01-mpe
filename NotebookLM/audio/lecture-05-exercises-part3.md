Talk through, in detail (3/3), the folk-theorem and bargaining exercises of the Lecture 5 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 05.pdf` (items 4, 8 and 10), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 05 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture06 - Bargaining and Bayesian Games.pdf` (frames 9–11 on the folk theorem, 13–21 on bargaining) and the books `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§10.6, Ch. 11), `Livro_Felix Munoz-Garcia, Daniel Toro-Gonzalez - Strategy and Game Theory Practice Exercises with Answers, 2nd ed. (2019, Springer).pdf` (Ch. 6) and `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§2.1.D). The listener has heard Parts 1 and 2 and has the statements in hand.

Item 4, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 6 Ex. 9, the folk theorem drawn. Stage game: (U, L) pays 9 each, (D, R) 7 each, and 10 against 1 off the diagonal. D and R are strictly dominant, so (D, R), paying (7, 7), is the only stage equilibrium. Part (b): measure repeated-game payoffs as averages, (1 minus delta) times the discounted sum, so that a constant stream of x is worth x and can be drawn in the stage-payoff plane. The feasible set is the convex hull of the four payoff pairs. Stress that (7, 7) lies strictly inside the triangle with corners (1, 10), (10, 1) and (9, 9), so the feasible set is that triangle. Part (c): the minimax is 7 for each player, equal to the stage Nash payoff. The feasible, individually rational set is the part of the triangle above 7 for both: a quadrilateral with corners (7, 7), (37/4, 7), (9, 9) and (7, 37/4), without its sides on the lines at 7. Part (d): grim trigger gives (9, 9) forever iff 9 over (1 minus delta) is at least 10 plus 7 delta over (1 minus delta), that is iff delta is at least one third. The same bound is necessary for any Nash equilibrium, because the punishment already holds the deviator to his minimax. Link this to the slides' message on frame 11: repetition makes cooperation possible, and almost anything else too, so the theorem explains rather than predicts. Traps: drawing the four points, or a quadrilateral with (7, 7) as a vertex, as the feasible set; using "at least 7" instead of "more than 7"; mixing present values and averages in one inequality.

Item 8, Tadelis 11.5 (Asymmetric Patience). Three periods, player 1 proposes first and last. Period 3: player 1 takes everything; player 2 must accept at indifference, otherwise player 1 has no best offer. Period 2: player 1 accepts any share of at least delta-one, so player 2 offers exactly delta-one. Period 1: player 2 accepts iff her share is at least delta-two times (1 minus delta-one), so player 1 keeps 1 minus delta-two times (1 minus delta-one), and agreement is immediate. With equal patience player 1's edge is (1 minus delta) squared plus delta squared, never below one half (reached at delta one half, where player 1 gets three quarters). Player 2 gets more than half iff delta-two times (1 minus delta-one) exceeds one half: she must be patient (delta-two above one half) and player 1 impatient. Example: delta-two 0.9 needs delta-one below 4/9; with delta-one 0.2 she gets 0.72. Traps: discounting player 2's continuation with player 1's factor; letting the responder reject at indifference; explaining (b) by "player 1 moves first" only, when he also moves last.

Item 10, Gibbons 2.20, Rubinstein's strategies. With s-star equal to 1 over (1 plus delta), note the two identities: 1 minus s-star equals delta s-star, and 1 minus delta s-star equals s-star. Nash part: against player 2's strategy, player 1 gets at most s-star, and his strategy gives exactly that; player 2 can get at most delta s-star, which accepting at once gives. Subgame perfection: check the four kinds of subgames (player 1 or player 2 proposing, player 1 or player 2 responding). Each responder accepts exactly when the offer is worth at least waiting, delta s-star, and each proposer's prescribed offer is best because a rejected offer yields only delta squared s-star. Then invoke the one-stage deviation principle. The first-mover advantage, (1 minus delta) over (1 plus delta), is the cost of one period of delay and vanishes as delta goes to 1 (Lecture 6, frame 20). Traps: proving only the Nash part or checking only the path; valuing a rejection at s-star instead of delta s-star; using the one-stage deviation principle without saying it needs discounting and bounded payoffs.

Close with the common thread: the future is what disciplines today, in a repeated game through punishments and in bargaining through the value of waiting.
