Explain and discuss in depth (2/2) Aula 2, "Bargaining": why private information breaks efficient agreement, and what bargaining costs. Use the uploaded source `Livro_Paul Milgrom, John Roberts - Economics, Organization, and Management (1992, Prentice-Hall, Inc.) - libgen.li.pdf` (M&R; Ch. 5 "Private Information and Precontractual Opportunism" and "Measurement Costs and Investments in Bargaining Position", pp. 140–149, with the Ch. 2 value maximization principle, pp. 35–38, assumed known from episode 1). Treat the listener as a graduate student: derive, do not summarise. Episode 1 showed that, with complete information, bargaining power moves only the transfers. Here the turn is M&R's sentence on p. 140: efficiency is relative to what is feasible, and what is feasible depends on who knows what.

Bargaining over a sale (pp. 140–142): set up Table 5.2. The seller's value is 0 or 2 (probabilities .8, .2) and the buyer's is 1 or 3 (.2, .8), so trade is efficient 96% of the time. Explain the temptation to misreport and the mediator device: if a neutral mediator cannot find prices that work, the parties cannot either. Walk through Table 5.3. A claimed value of 2 forces a price of 2 (participation), and a claimed value of 1 forces a price of 1. The seller's honesty constraint, .2 × 1 + .8p ≥ 1.60, gives p ≥ 1.75. The buyer's, 2.60 − .8p ≥ 1.60, gives p ≤ 1.25. No price satisfies both. Define the informational rent (fn. 7) and explain why the revelation principle (fn. 8) makes this mediator argument general. Then the reversed probabilities: at p = 1.50 honesty pays the seller (1.10 against 0.40). Draw the intuition: lying is cheap when the trade is likely to happen anyway.

Incentive efficiency (pp. 143–145): give the definition: no feasible mechanism, given the incentive constraints, that everyone prefers. Run the split-the-difference example, with values uniform on [0,1]. Use M&R's numbers to show that a small overstatement pays the seller (gain .0048, loss .0001). Then derive from fn. 9 the linear equilibrium b = 1/12 + (2/3)B, s = 1/4 + (2/3)S, so trade happens only if B > S + 1/4. Have the hosts verify aloud that trade then occurs with probability 9/32 instead of 1/2, yet captures 27/32 (about 84%) of the expected gains, because only the small-gain trades are lost. Describe in words a short Python Monte Carlo that would confirm both numbers. Stress M&R's claim that these rules are incentive efficient: longer haggling only turns the loss into delay.

Many participants (pp. 145–147): a public project costs 1 and each person values it at 2 with probability p. With two people, the honesty constraint p(2 − .5) + (1 − p)(2 − 1) ≥ 2p gives p ≤ 2/3. With a build probability q when only one person volunteers, q must fall toward 3/4 as p → 1. With three people the constraint needs p below about .44. At N = 3 and p = 1/2, the project can be built less than 90% of the time when only one person speaks up, even though that person alone would pay for it (p. 146). With many people, the chance that anyone volunteers tends to about two in seven. Explain the mechanism: a misreport is punished only when you are pivotal, and pivotality vanishes as N grows. Connect it to free riding.

Bargaining costs and bargaining position (pp. 147–149): bounded rationality makes reaching agreement costly even without strategy, and private information adds delay and breakdown. So, in M&R's words, simple value maximization computed without the incentive constraints is "a misleading basis for prediction" (p. 147). Measurement costs: when both sides inspect a good of unknown quality, the price moves but total wealth does not, so the inspection is pure waste. Tell the De Beers story in full: graded sights, a take-it-or-leave-it formula price, no negotiation, and exclusion for refusing. Argue M&R's value-maximizing reading against the plain monopoly reading, then extend it to block booking, prepackaged produce, warranties and royalties. Finish with bargaining chips (the Strategic Defense Initiative) and the waste of haggling and posturing. Tie this back to episode 1: these are exactly the transaction costs the Coase theorem assumed away.

Push harder: note that Table 5.3 is a discrete case of a general impossibility result for bilateral trade (Myerson–Satterthwaite; the name is not in M&R). Ask why an incentive-efficient outcome is not a failure of rationality. Address misconceptions: the revelation principle does not say people tell the truth; it says honest-reporting mechanisms lose nothing in generality. De Beers' rules are not explained by monopoly power alone. More participants make agreement harder, not easier. End by pointing ahead: the same private information shuts down whole markets under adverse selection (M&R p. 149 onward, Aula 10).
