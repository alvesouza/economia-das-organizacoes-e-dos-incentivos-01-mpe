Talk through, in detail (2/3), the Lecture 1 real-list exercises that ask you to build a game from rules and to prove a property of dominance: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 01.pdf` (items 4, 6 and 7), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 01 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture01 - Static Games of Complete Information I.pdf` (slides 13–19 on the normal form, 23–29 on dominance) and the Tadelis book (Ch. 3, §4.1–4.2). The listener has heard Part 1 and has the statements in hand.

Anchor from the slides: a normal-form game has three ingredients, the players, each player's strategy set and each player's payoff function (slides 13–14). Payoffs are utilities over outcomes (slide 18); when a coin or a probability decides who wins, as in items 6 and 7, the payoff is the expected value. Most errors in building a matrix come from filling a cell the rules do not state directly.

Item 4, Tadelis 4.1, a short proof: a strictly dominant strategy equilibrium, when it exists, is the only dominant strategy equilibrium. Argue one player at a time. Suppose player i's strategy s is strictly dominant and some other strategy t were also dominant, even weakly. Strict dominance says s pays strictly more than t against every profile of the others. Dominance of t would say t pays at least as much as s against every profile. Pick any one profile, which exists because strategy sets are non-empty: both cannot hold. So t is not dominant, and any other profile must differ from the strict one in some player's strategy, which is then not dominant. Add the remarks: the result does not promise existence (Battle of the Sexes has none), and the strict profile is also the only IESDS survivor and the only Nash equilibrium. Traps: ruling out only a second strict equilibrium, when the claim covers weakly dominant ones too; comparing whole profiles instead of one player's strategies; skipping the non-empty step.

Item 6, Tadelis 4.7, the campaign game. Strategies are positive, balanced or negative. The book gives four win probabilities: one half when both choose the same, 0 for positive against balanced, 0.3 for positive against negative, 0.6 for negative against balanced. The key modelling step is to fill the missing cells with complementarity, because exactly one candidate wins: balanced beats positive with probability 1, negative beats positive with 0.7, and balanced against negative wins with 0.4. Player 2's payoff is 1 minus player 1's: the game is symmetric and constant-sum. Round 1: positive is dominated by both balanced and negative. Balanced is not, because against positive it is the unique best reply. Round 2: on the columns balanced and negative, negative pays (0.6, 0.5) against balanced's (0.5, 0.4), so balanced goes. The unique prediction is that both go negative, each winning with probability one half, also the unique Nash equilibrium. Interpretation: the candidates end up where two positive campaigns would have left them, so for voters who dislike negativity it is a prisoner's-dilemma outcome. Traps: filling the missing cells by symmetry, giving balanced against positive a probability of 0 instead of 1; deleting balanced in round 1; giving only the matrix when part (a) asks for players, strategy sets and payoff functions.

Item 7, Tadelis 4.3, a first-price auction with bids 0, 1 or 2. Player 1 values the item at 3 and player 2 at 5. Only the winner pays his bid, and a tie is settled by a fair coin, so each tied bidder gets one half of value minus bid. Build the cells: a tie at 0 gives (1.5, 2.5), at 1 gives (1, 2), at 2 gives (0.5, 1.5). Part (b): player 2's bid 0 is strictly dominated by bid 2. Player 1 has no pure strategy strictly dominated by another pure strategy: his bid 0 is only weakly dominated by bid 1. Note the remark in the solutions, though: a mix of three quarters on bid 1 and one quarter on bid 2 would strictly dominate it, which Chapter 4's pure definition does not count. Part (c) takes four rounds, alternating between players: player 2's 0, then player 1's 0, then player 2's 1, then player 1's 1. Only both bidding 2 survives, with expected payoffs (0.5, 1.5). Point out the inefficiency: the bidder with the lower value gets the item half the time. Traps: giving the tied winner the full surplus instead of the coin-toss expectation; charging the loser his bid; stopping after round 1 or 2.

Close with the lesson: write every payoff from the rules, check that the rules are consistent with each other, and only then start deleting.
