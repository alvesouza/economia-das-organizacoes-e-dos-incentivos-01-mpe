Talk through, in detail (2/3), the finite Bayesian-game exercises of the Lecture 6 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 06.pdf` (items 1, 4, 5 and 7), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 06 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture06 - Bargaining and Bayesian Games.pdf` (frames 23–36) and the Tadelis (Ch. 12) and Gibbons (§3.1) books. The listener has heard Part 1 on bargaining and has the statements in hand. Reason aloud; do not just announce equilibria.

Open with the slides' anchor, the Munich Agreement. Hitler's type is friendly with probability 0.6 or belligerent with 0.4. Each type best-responds: the friendly type accommodates a concession and goes to war otherwise; the belligerent type always goes to war. Chamberlain compares 2.2 with 2 and concedes. Stress the lesson: with hindsight conceding looks wrong, but it was a best response to his beliefs. Then state the slides' three-step recipe: (1) best action of each type of each player; (2) uninformed players maximize expected payoffs with their beliefs; (3) everyone best-responds at once.

Item 1, Gibbons 3.1 (definitions). A static Bayesian game lists players, type spaces, a common prior, action sets and payoffs that depend on actions and types. A pure strategy is a complete plan: a function from one's own types to actions. A Bayesian Nash equilibrium requires every type, not just the realized one, to best-respond to the others' strategies, using the conditional probability of the others' types given its own. Traps: giving a strategy as a single action; checking only the realized type; best-responding to the rivals' realized actions instead of their strategies; forgetting that the prior is common knowledge.

Item 4, Gibbons 3.4 (Nature picks Game 1 or Game 2). Player 1 has four strategies, one action for each game, and player 2 has two. Player 2's payoff from L is one half if player 1 plays T in Game 1; from R it is 1 if player 1 plays B in Game 2. Go through the candidates and arrive at three pure BNE: (TT, L), (TB, R) and (BB, R). Explain why the first and the third survive: one type of player 1 is indifferent, because all its payoffs against that column are zero, and indifference is enough for a best response. Traps: discarding profiles in which a type is indifferent; solving the two games separately and calling the pair an equilibrium; letting player 2 condition on the game she cannot see.

Item 5, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 7 Ex. 1. Complete information first: with x equal to 15 there are two equilibria, (A, C) and (B, D); with x equal to 4 only (B, D). Then build the Bayesian normal form with four rows for player 1 (an action for x = 15 and an action for x = 4) and two columns. The key computation: against "A if 15, B if 4", player 2 gets 9 from C (half of 18 plus half of 0) and 9 from D (half of 7 plus half of 11), an exact tie. The BNE are (A if 15, B if 4; C) and (B always; D). Add the comparative static: C remains a best reply iff the probability of x = 15 is at least one half. Traps: averaging player 2's payoff while holding player 1's action fixed across types; writing only two rows; throwing away the tie.

Item 7, Tadelis 12.3 (Armed Conflict), two-sided private types. Write each army's payoff carefully: attacking an army that does not attack captures the territory with no cost; when both attack, a strong army beats a weak one, gaining m minus s while the weak army loses w; with equal strength neither captures and both pay their cost. Strategies are pairs (action if strong, action if weak). For m = 3, w = 2, s = 1 the pure BNE are (AA, AN) and (AN, AA): one army always attacks and the other attacks only when strong. Verify one fully: the weak type facing an always-attacking rival gets minus 2 from attacking, so it stays out. For m = 3, w = 4, s = 2 the BNE are (AN, AN), (AA, NN) and (NN, AA). In (AN, AN) a strong army attacking expects one half times minus 2 plus one half times 3, which is 0.5; a weak one expects minus 0.5 and stays out. Interpret why higher fighting costs make "attack only when strong" an equilibrium. Traps: giving m minus w to an army in an equal-strength fight; charging fighting costs when only one side attacks; looking only for symmetric equilibria; fixing the rival's action instead of averaging over its types.

Close with the outsourcing application as a check on the recipe. Each supplier type has a dominant action: the responsible type plays R and the irresponsible type plays I. The multinational hires iff 4p minus 2 is at least minus p, so iff p is at least 2/5. Reputation, certification and audits raise p.
