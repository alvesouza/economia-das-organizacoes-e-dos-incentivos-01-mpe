Explain and discuss in detail the Lecture 3 case: Görges (2021), "Of housewives and feminists", read as a battle of the sexes. Use the uploaded sources `Caso_Gorges 2021 - Of housewives and feminists.pdf` (the paper), `Caso_Gorges 2021 - game-theory solution.pdf` (the worked case), `Aula_Lecture03 - Mixed Strategies and Dynamic Games.pdf` (mixed BoS, sequential BoS, subgame perfection) and `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Ch. 6 and Ch. 7–8). The listener is an economics master's student who has heard Lectures 1–3. Reason aloud through every argument instead of listing results, and keep every number exact.

The design: 192 subjects, real heterosexual couples in Hamburg, play a one-shot game with no communication, once with their partner and once with a stranger. The Neutral group sees actions labelled A and B, the Norm group sees Career and Family. Payoffs: if one partner picks Career and the other Family, the Career player gets 200 and the Family player 100; if both pick the same, both get 0. Choices are revealed to the partner afterwards. Ask why a one-shot, simultaneous game is a deliberate model of non-cooperative bargaining over the division of labour, and what it rules out by design: productivity differences, preferences for childcare, and redistribution between strangers.

The equilibria: name the two pure equilibria, traditional (man Career, woman Family) and progressive. Derive the mixed equilibrium by the principle of indifference, as in the Lecture 3 slides: if the partner picks Career with probability q, Career pays (1 − q) times 200 and Family pays q times 100, so indifference gives q = 2/3. Each player gets 200/3, about 66.7. Coordination happens with probability 4/9, the paper's "just below 45%", split 2/9 traditional and 2/9 progressive, with both-Career at 4/9 and both-Family at 1/9. Contrast 300 total in a pure equilibrium with 133 in the mixed one.

Focal points: explain Schelling's idea that a label can select an equilibrium without changing any payoff. Predict what the Norm framing should do: more traditional coordination, less mis-coordination, efficiency up and equality down.

The evidence: women choose Career at 56% in Neutral and 25% in Norm; men at 54% and 63%. The marginal effects of Norm: progressive equilibrium minus 17 points, traditional plus 25.5, mis-coordination minus 9 and not significant. Earnings in Norm: men 112.50, women 75, so women earn 67% of men; in Neutral the two are about equal. Then the sharp point. Against the men's actual 62.5% Career rate, a woman in Norm earns 75 from Career and 62.5 from Family, so Career was her best response; her beliefs overshot the men's real rate. Ask the listener what that says about equilibrium beliefs.

The own-payoff puzzle, flagged as the case's own analysis and not the paper's: suppose the norm gives women an identity bonus n for choosing Family. In the mixed equilibrium a player's own payoffs pin down the opponent's mix, so the men's Career probability falls to (200 − n)/300 while the women stay at 2/3. The data move the women, not the men. Argue why this favours a focal-point selection story over a payoff shift inside the mixed equilibrium.

Men "acting feminist": non-committed men facing their partner in Norm pick Career only 33% of the time, against 78% with strangers. With a belief of 0.33 that the partner chooses Career, Career pays 134 and Family 33, so the signal to a potential long-term partner must be worth more than 101 ECU, about one euro. Present it as a preview of signalling, and as the paper's suggestive, not designed, finding.

The dynamic view from Lecture 3: make the game sequential. Whoever moves first, the one who takes the job offer or decides first after a birth, gets 200 in the subgame-perfect equilibrium. Show the Nash equilibrium of the sequential game that rests on a non-credible threat and why backward induction kills it. Connect this to the paper's footnote 12: specialisation feeds back into wages, so the first outcome locks in. Give a short preview of Lecture 5: in the repeated game couples could alternate and get 150 per period each, but path dependence makes alternation costly.

Inequality aversion: derive the paper's footnote 13 with Fehr–Schmidt preferences. Coordination survives for the advantaged player if the guilt weight is below 2, and for the disadvantaged player if the envy weight is below 1.

Close with the limits (student sample, small stakes, one shot, subgroups of 22 to 30 committed couples) and one question to keep thinking about: when a society's norm is a coordination device, what policy changes the focal point rather than the payoffs?
