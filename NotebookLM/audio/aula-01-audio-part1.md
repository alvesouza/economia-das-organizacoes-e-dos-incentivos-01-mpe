Explain and discuss in depth (1/2) Aula 1, "Does Organization Matter? Ford × GM × Toyota": the cases and what they prove. Use the uploaded sources `Livro_Paul Milgrom, John Roberts - Economics, Organization, and Management (1992, Prentice-Hall, Inc.) - libgen.li.pdf` (M&R; Ch. 1, printed pp. 2–18, and Ch. 4 p. 109), `Aula_01_notes - Does Organization Matter.md` (the class notes, §1–§5) and `Livro_Luis M. B. Cabral - Introduction to Industrial Organization, second edition (2017, MIT Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (§3.5, pp. 56–59). Treat the listener as a graduate student: argue, do not summarise. Episode 2 does the mathematics; here, set up every claim it will formalise.

The puzzle: open with M&R's thesis that organization and strategy can matter as much as technology, cost and demand (p. 16). Ford had technology and scale yet lost to GM; GM lost share to a smaller, capital-poor Toyota. Ask what kind of evidence could support that claim, and why market shares alone cannot.

Ford as a coherent system: one model, dedicated equipment, disciplined labour paid by the job, over 50% of the market (p. 109); 55% in 1921 against 11% for all GM brands (p. 2). Walk through how each choice raised the value of the others, using the table in the notes §1, and why the 1920 price cut worked only while buyers wanted one standard car.

GM and Sloan: the 1920–21 crisis (sales down 75%, inventories never charged to divisions), the segmented-market strategy, and the multidivisional form (pp. 2–4): operating autonomy for divisions, a central office that audits, plans strategy and checks internal prices against market prices. Stress M&R's point that variety multiplied the decisions *and* the need for coordination, so neither Ford's organization nor the old GM could carry the strategy. Quote Henry Ford's "great big chart" and give the outcome (1927–37 losses vs. profits; 45% vs. 16% shares in 1940). Then show that GM's system was coherent too: shared components across models paid for specialized equipment, full use of efficient parts plants justified vertical integration, and suppliers were made to compete on price (pp. 5–6). The cost was rigidity: major redesigns only about every 12 years in the 1950s and 1960s.

Toyota: derive each practice from Toyota's constraints (pp. 4–6): just-in-time because inventory systems have scale economies Toyota lacked; then reliability, operators repairing machines, flexible equipment, frequent redesign, few long-term outside suppliers. Read M&R p. 17 aloud: low inventories required reliability, quick repair and close supplier communication. Contrast supplier relations directly: GM integrated and switched business among many suppliers; Toyota relied on a few outside suppliers for complex systems such as brakes and fuel injection, in long-term relations that made them willing to invest in Toyota-specific skills and machines, and redesigned twice as often. Add Cabral p. 57 on why so many aspects made the system hard to pin down and copy, and connect to causal ambiguity.

The supporting cases, each isolating one mechanism: Hudson's Bay vs. North West Company (pp. 6–9: decision rights plus profit shares beat a 2-to-1 cost advantage; HBC recovered by copying the organization), Salomon Brothers (pp. 9–12: individual bonuses that kill cooperation; the 1990 stock-in-trust reform and its 7–10% market test), and central planning (pp. 12–16): explain the ratchet effect formally as a dynamic incentive problem (exceeding quota raises future quotas, so capacity is hidden), and the "only the trucks changed sides" joke as an argument about interdependent reforms.

The four patterns (pp. 16–17): organization matters; incentives; authority placed where the information is; and the key sentence, "incentives and delegated authority are complements: each makes the other more valuable". Then the frame of the course: coordination (information, making plans fit) vs. motivation (interests), with each case placed in both columns as in the notes §5.

Push harder: discuss whether the cases establish causation or only illustrate it (selection, survivorship, the efficiency-as-positive-principle view previewed in M&R Ch. 2, p. 24); why "fit" is a claim about cross effects rather than about each practice being good; and why the ratchet effect returns in Aula 16 (Ch. 7, p. 232). Address misconceptions explicitly: organization is not the org chart (p. 16); Ford was not irrational, it was coherent for its time; and a better system that a rival can watch every day is not thereby easy to copy. End by stating the question for episode 2: if choices are complements, why can a firm see a better system and still fail to reach it?
