Talk through, in detail (3/3), the three hardest Lecture 1 real-list exercises: elimination over a continuum of strategies and why the order of elimination does not matter. Solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 01.pdf` (items 8, 9 and 10), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 01 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture01 - Static Games of Complete Information I.pdf` (slides 21–22 on existence, uniqueness and invariance, slides 28–29 on IESDS), the Tadelis book (§4.2.2, Cournot by IESDS) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§8.B). The listener has heard Parts 1 and 2 and has the statements in hand.

The tool for continuous games: payoffs of the form b times q times (2x minus q) are parabolas with their peak at x. Comparing two actions factorizes, and an action above every possible peak is strictly dominated by the highest peak, while an action below every peak is dominated by the lowest one. So in each round, find the range of best replies to the opponent's surviving actions, and delete everything outside it.

Item 8, Tadelis 4.6, the roommates who clean. The payoff is (10 minus t_j) times t_i minus t_i squared. The first-order condition gives the best reply 5 minus t_j over 2, or 0 when t_j is 10 or more. It decreases, so cleaning is a strategic substitute. Round 1: best replies never exceed 5, so any time above 5 is dominated by 5, and every time between 0 and 5 is the unique best reply to something. Then iterate. The opponent's upper bound gives your lower bound, because the best reply decreases: the intervals run from 0 to 5, then 2.5 to 5, 2.5 to 3.75, 3.125 to 3.75, 3.125 to 3.4375. The width halves each round and the midpoint converges to 10 over 3, so IESDS predicts 10 over 3 hours each, also the Nash equilibrium. Each gets 100 over 9. Cleaning 2.5 hours each would give 12.5, because each roommate ignores the negative effect of his cleaning on the other. Traps: answering round 1 with 0 to 10, where the payoff is positive, instead of the best-reply range; pairing lower bound with lower bound in the recursion; claiming an action is dominated without naming the dominating action, which is the nearer endpoint.

Item 9, MWG 8.B.4, a proof: in a finite game, the set that survives iterated strict dominance does not depend on the order of deletion. Build it from three lemmas. One: dominance is inherited, because an inequality that holds against every opponent profile still holds when fewer profiles remain. Two: a dominated strategy has a dominator that is itself undominated. Strict dominance is a strict partial order, so the finite set of dominators has a maximal element. Three: the end point of any deletion order is contained in every stage of any other order. If one of its strategies were deleted at some stage, inheritance makes it dominated in the end point by an undominated dominator that is never deleted, a contradiction. Apply Lemma 3 in both directions and the two end points coincide. Then show why the hypotheses matter: with weak dominance, the order changes the answer (the solutions give a three-by-two example ending at payoffs (2, 1) or (1, 1)); with a continuum, one player with payoff s on the interval below 1 and 0 at 1 can end at the empty set or at {0, 1}. Traps: saying "a dominated strategy stays dominated" without checking that its dominator survives; proving only that deleting everything at once removes more; extending the result to weak dominance or infinite games.

Item 10, MWG 8.B.5, Cournot by IESDS. Let m be the monopoly output, (a minus c) over 2b; the best reply is m minus the rivals' total over 2. With two firms, the rounds give 0 to m, m over 2 to m, m over 2 to three quarters m, five eighths m to three quarters m, and so on. The width halves and the intervals shrink to two thirds of m, that is (a minus c) over 3b, the Cournot output. With three firms, round 1 still leaves 0 to m. But the two rivals together can now produce up to 2m, which makes zero a best reply, so every output in 0 to m is a best reply to some surviving pair and round 2 deletes nothing. IESDS gives no unique prediction, although the Nash equilibrium, m over 2 each, is still unique. Traps: answering part (a) with "the best replies cross", which is the Nash argument and not dominance; bounding the rivals' total by m instead of 2m; concluding that the three-firm game has many Nash equilibria.

Close by linking back to the criteria of slides 21–22. In finite games IESDS always leaves something (existence), and item 9 adds that its answer does not depend on the order of deletion. But uniqueness is not guaranteed, as item 10 shows, and Lecture 2 adds correct beliefs to get it.
