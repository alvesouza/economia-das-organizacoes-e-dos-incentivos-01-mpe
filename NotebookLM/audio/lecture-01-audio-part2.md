Explain and describe in detail (2/2) Lecture 1, "Static games of complete information I": iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS) and its application to spatial competition. Use the uploaded sources `Aula_Lecture01 - Static Games of Complete Information I.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis §4.2 and §5.2.5), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §1.1.B and §1.2.A), the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§8.B), `Livro_Felix Munoz-Garcia, Daniel Toro-Gonzalez - Strategy and Game Theory Practice Exercises with Answers, 2nd ed. (2019, Springer).pdf` (Ch. 1) and the Cabral `Introduction to Industrial Organization` source (§14.2). The listener has heard Part 1 (normal form, common knowledge, dominance, the prisoner's dilemma).

From one rational player to common knowledge of rationality: start with the slides' chain. A rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy; if everyone knows this, each can ignore others' dominated strategies; if everyone knows that everyone knows, the next layer goes too. Make the key point explicit: a removal in round k relies on k minus 1 levels of "knows that the other is rational", so a procedure that stops after K rounds needs K minus 1 levels. Common knowledge covers every finite game. Use Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 1 Ex. 12 to separate what rationality alone removes from what common knowledge removes.

The recursion: put the slides' definition in words: S_i^0 = S_i, then S_i^k keeps the strategies not strictly dominated within the surviving sets, and S_i^infinity is the intersection over all rounds. Any surviving profile is Tadelis's "iterated-elimination equilibrium". Walk through the slides' three-by-three example from Tadelis §4.2.1: why no player has a dominant strategy, which strategy falls first and why, and how each removal creates the next. Note Tadelis's remark that the survivor is not Pareto optimal.

Properties to discuss carefully. First, Tadelis Prop. 4.2: a strict dominant-strategy equilibrium is the unique survivor, because everything else falls in round one. Second, order independence: under strict dominance the final set does not depend on the order or speed of removal, since a strictly dominated strategy stays dominated as opponents' strategies disappear; under weak dominance different orders can leave different survivors (MWG exercise 8.B.4, Muñoz-Garcia Ex. 16). Third, existence: in finite games S^infinity is never empty, because a best reply to some surviving profile is never strictly dominated. Fourth, uniqueness often fails: in a pure coordination game nothing is removed. Grade IESDS on Tadelis §4.2.3's criteria and contrast it with dominant-strategy equilibrium: it exists more often, but at the cost of uniqueness.

Cournot by elimination: with linear inverse demand P = a minus b times total output and marginal cost c, reason through Gibbons §1.2.A and Tadelis §4.2. Round one removes every output above the monopoly output (a minus c) over 2b. Round two removes outputs below the best reply to that, (a minus c) over 4b. The bounds then alternate and converge to (a minus c) over 3b, the unique survivor. Intuition: each best reply moves half as much as the rival's output. Then the twist from MWG exercise 8.B.5: with three or more firms, the rivals' joint output can reach (a minus c) over b. Zero output then becomes a best reply, and elimination stops after one round. This motivates Nash equilibrium in Lecture 2.

Spatial competition: present the slides' motivation, competing pharmacies opening side by side in Brasília, and the mini case's assumptions. These are a line of equally populated blocks, simultaneous location choice, identical prices and products, customers going to the nearest store with even splits, and firms maximizing customers. The mini case is graded in-class work: do not solve it, and do not say which locations survive it or which pair minimizes walking. Instead discuss the method: how to write a location payoff piecewise around the midpoint between two stores, why checking that one location dominates another requires every rival location (including the same block), and why the firms' payoffs are constant-sum. Explain that customer welfare (total walking distance) is a different objective from the firms' (customers captured), so compare the elimination outcome with the welfare optimum, and say why a median minimizes total distance for one facility. Finally, discuss what changes when firms also choose prices, using Cabral §14.2 on Hotelling's linear city, and give real examples of clustering and of spreading out.

Close with the lecture's arc. Dominance needs no beliefs, IESDS needs common knowledge of rationality, and when IESDS leaves too much, beliefs must be pinned down: best responses, rationalizability and Nash equilibrium (Tadelis §4.3 and Ch. 5, Lecture 2).
