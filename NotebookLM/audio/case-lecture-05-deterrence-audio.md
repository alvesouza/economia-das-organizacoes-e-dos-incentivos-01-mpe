Explain and discuss in detail the Lecture 5 case: Maheshri and Mastrobuoni (2021), "The race between deterrence and displacement", read as a coordination game among banks, first static, then repeated. Use the uploaded sources `Caso_Maheshri Mastrobuoni 2021 - The race between deterrence and displacement.pdf` (the paper), `Caso_Maheshri Mastrobuoni 2021 - game-theory solution.pdf` (the worked case), `Aula_Lecture05 - Repeated Games.pdf` (carrots and sticks, one-stage deviation, grim trigger), `Aula_Lecture04 - Credible Threats and Subgame Perfection.pdf` (subgame perfection, observability) and `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Ch. 9–10). The listener is a master's student who has heard Lectures 1–5. Reason aloud; keep numbers exact.

The game: each bank in a market chooses whether to hire a guard, which costs c. A robbery costs it L. Its robbery probability depends on its own guard and on the share of guarded neighbours. Deterrence: a guard lowers your own risk. Displacement: your neighbours' guards push robbers onto you. Robbers are not modelled as players, but displacement is their best response. A bank hires if the guard premium, the drop in its own robbery probability, exceeds c over L. The key fact is that the premium rises as more neighbours hire, because displaced crime falls on unguarded banks. That makes guards strategic complements, so expect multiple equilibria.

The calibration from the paper's 500-metre estimates: base risk 7%, a guard cuts it by 3.18 points, a fully guarded neighbourhood adds 1.69 points to an unguarded bank, and the interaction is minus 3.36. So the premium is 0.0318 with no guarded neighbours and 0.0654 with all neighbours guarded, and 53% of deterred robberies are displaced, the paper's "over half".

The two-bank game, with a guard at 40,000 euros: if L is below about 612,000 nobody hires; above about 1,258,000 hiring is dominant; in between both "no guards" and "both guards" are equilibria. Walk through L equal to one million, with expected costs in thousands: 70 each with no guards, 61.5 each with both, 78.2 for the lone guarded bank and 86.9 for its unguarded neighbour. That is a stag hunt; the mixed equilibrium hires with probability 0.244. Then L equal to 700,000: 49 each with no guards against 55.05 with both, so here the guarded equilibrium is the bad one. This is the paper's Proposition 2: despite a negative externality, the market can have too few guards or too many. Note that the payoff-dominance cutoff, 0.0485, and the risk-dominance cutoff, 0.0486, nearly coincide.

Many banks: explain the ordering lemma (a bank hires only if every lower-cost bank hires), Proposition 1's threshold equilibria, and Proposition 3, complete coordination. Flag a detail in the paper: its thresholds of 1.37 million and 662,000 euros reproduce with a 44,000-euro guard and the Table 4 column 2 estimates, not with the 40,000 stated in the text.

A four-bank example with cost ratios 0.020, 0.044, 0.050 and 0.068. Only two configurations are equilibria: bank 1 alone, with total cost 290,154, and banks 1 to 3, with 271,510. The social optimum is all four guarded, at 252,393, but bank 4 saves 1,529 by firing its guard. A ban gives 300,813; a requirement gives the optimum. A subsidy of 1,600 per guard also makes all four the unique equilibrium here, but only because the cost ratios sit near the thresholds. Connect this to the paper's argument that quantity rules fix coordination failures better than taxes.

The repeated game, the heart of the Lecture 5 link: treat the 2000–2009 panel as a game repeated ten times with observed guards. First show with the one-stage deviation principle that any sequence of stage equilibria is subgame perfect. Then build the carrot and stick from the Lecture 5 slides. In year 1 all four hire. In year 2 the banks play the bank-1-alone equilibrium if bank 4 complied, a cost of 44,490 to bank 4, and banks 1 to 3 if it fired, 51,118. This sustains year 1 if the discount factor is at least 1,529 over 6,628, about 0.23. Stress the twist: the carrot for an unguarded bank is fewer guards around it. Then explain why Nash reversion cannot sustain all four forever: both stage equilibria cost bank 4 less than the 52,647 it pays when everyone is guarded, so there is no stick that hurts it.

Observability, from Lecture 4: guards are visible. Contrast hidden LoJack trackers, which protect every car, with visible devices, which divert thieves.

The econometrics as game theory: explain why the naive regression of robberies on guards finds almost nothing (minus 0.0043) until market fixed effects absorb the common environment (about minus 0.044 at 5 km), and why simultaneous choices by neighbours are a reflection problem.

Close with the policy result (limits in rural markets, requirements in dense cities) and one open question: who in a real market can play the coordinating role that the repeated game cannot?
