Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Lecture 1, "Static games of complete information I": representing a game and solving it by dominance. Use the uploaded sources `Aula_Lecture01 - Static Games of Complete Information I.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis, the book the slides follow: Ch. 3 and §4.1), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §1.1.A) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§7.D, §8.B) for the formal side. The listener is an economics master's student who knows consumer theory but not game theory. Keep every claim precise, and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

What game theory adds: the slides define it by models, rationality and interdependence. Explain why interdependence is the break with single-agent optimization: each payoff depends on everyone's actions, so a choice requires beliefs about others, whose choices depend on beliefs about you. Name this "reasoning about reasoning" and say it is the thread of the whole course.

The normal form: build G = (I, {S_i}, {u_i}) from Tadelis Def. 3.3, then the notation S as a Cartesian product, a profile s, s_{-i} and (s_i, s_{-i}). Explain why a strategy is a complete contingent plan, using the slides' chess example, and why this lets any dynamic game be written in normal form. Work out aloud that a second mover with k actions who observes one of m first-mover actions has k to the power m strategies.

Static versus dynamic, complete versus incomplete: separate the two distinctions carefully. Static means not observing others' moves; complete information means the game itself is common knowledge. Give Tadelis Def. 3.1's infinite hierarchy of "I know that you know", and contrast a private letter each player reads with a public announcement. Flag the common confusion: simultaneous Cournot with known costs is static and complete, not incomplete.

Outcomes versus payoffs, the grade game: describe the slides' alpha/beta grade bids and explain why the outcome matrix is not yet a game. Have the hosts suppose a player values her own grade plus lambda times her partner's grade, and reason through how lambda decides whether alpha is strictly dominant, whether beta is, or neither. Stress that dominance only compares payoffs strategy by strategy, so it needs ordinal preferences; expected utility enters later with mixed strategies.

Judging a solution concept: Tadelis §3.3.2 gives existence, uniqueness and invariance to small payoff changes. Explain each, and why uniqueness is hard when best actions depend on others.

Dominance: state strict and weak dominance with their quantifier, "for all s_{-i}", and explain why that quantifier makes dominance belief-free. Present the strict dominant-strategy equilibrium and prove Tadelis Prop. 4.1, that it is unique, by the two-line contradiction. Then grade the concept on the three criteria: rarely exists, unique when it does, invariant because strict inequalities have slack. Explain why weak dominance is weaker: a weakly dominated strategy can tie for best against some profile, so rationality alone does not exclude it. That is why elimination in Part 2 uses strict dominance only.

Social dilemmas and Pareto optimality: walk through the slides' mum/fink prisoner's dilemma (Tadelis §3.2.1) and state Lesson 1, a rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy. Define Pareto domination and optimality (Tadelis Def. 3.5), and show which profiles are Pareto optimal in a general prisoner's dilemma with T > R > P > S. Make clear that Pareto optimality is not maximizing the sum. Then Lesson 2: individually rational choices can produce socially bad outcomes. Apply it to the slides' partnership game: effort costs c and adds y to output, which is split equally. Reason why effort is dominant exactly when c is below y over 2, and why the dilemma region, y/2 < c < y, widens to y/n < c < y with n partners: each member keeps only 1/n of what their own effort creates. Connect this to Milgrom and Roberts' theme that organization exists to fix such incentive problems, and add that binding contracts with side payments would change the game itself.

The second-price auction: using the slides' example, reason through why bidding one's value weakly dominates every other bid. Underbidding only matters when the highest rival bid lies between the bid and the value, and overbidding only when it lies between the value and the bid. Explain why the dominance is weak, not strict, and preview its return in Tadelis Ch. 13.
