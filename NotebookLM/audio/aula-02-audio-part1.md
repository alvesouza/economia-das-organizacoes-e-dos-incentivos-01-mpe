Treat the listener as a graduate student who has heard Aula 1: argue, do not summarise. Episode 2 adds private information; here everyone knows everyone's values.

The puzzle: economics long called small-numbers bargaining indeterminate, a matter of strength, patience and guile (M&R p. 140). Open with M&R's answer: without wealth effects, what gets created is determinate; only who gets it is left to bargaining power.

No wealth effects: state M&R's three conditions (p. 35): a definite compensating amount exists, it does not depend on wealth, and the person can afford to pay it. Derive the value index u(x,y) = x + v(y) (p. 36) and show the transfer that compensates a switch from y1 to y2 is v(y1) − v(y2) at any initial wealth. Discuss where this fails: the lottery winner, the worker liable for a reactor accident.

Value maximization principle (p. 36): derive it for two investors. Since x1 + x2 = P(y), total value P(y) + v1(y) + v2(y) depends on y alone; walk through Figure 2.1's 45-degree lines and why any point on a lower line is Pareto dominated. Stress the separation of value creation from distribution, and the footnote's limit: with wealth effects, maximizing the sum is sufficient for efficiency but not necessary. Point to M&R Q 2.1–2.2 (the shared beach) and Q 2.3 (the proof).

The Coase theorem (p. 38): state it, then its corollary that the efficient organization minimizes transaction costs. Run Cabral's steel plant and downstream residents (pp. 97–98): give the rights to either side and the dumping decision is the same; only the payments change. Add Cabral's efficiency-versus-fairness point and Varian §35.2 (quasilinear preferences). Then M&R's warning (p. 39): the theorem needs unlimited transfers, so it fits a GM–Toyota contract but not land tenure or slavery. Contrast the transaction-cost view with the two rivals M&R name (p. 39). Against the Marxian view: Yugoslav worker-run firms hired managers who organized like capitalist firms. Against the Harvard school: resale price maintenance as protection against free riding on dealer service, not as collusion.

Who gets what: mention Nash bargaining as Varian frames it (axiomatic; the outcome turns on risk aversion and the disagreement point). Then the strategic route: solve Varian's three-day Alice–Bob game (pp. 581–582) by backward induction: Alice gets 1 − β(1 − α), Bob β(1 − α). Then the infinite horizon: (1 − β)/(1 − αβ) and β(1 − α)/(1 − αβ). Link to MWG Example 9.AA.1 (finite horizon: who offers last matters) and 9.AA.2 (Rubinstein: unique SPNE, share v/(1 + δ) with a common δ). Describe in words, not code, a short Python loop that solves the T-period game backwards and shows the shares converging. The moral: patience and the right to propose are bargaining power; they move x, never y, and agreement is immediate, so nothing is wasted.

Commitment: M&R p. 133 (William the Conqueror and Cortes burning their fleets; Apple's single-purpose Macintosh plant) and p. 139 (commitment by non-contractual means is costly; reputation can substitute, and discretion belongs with the party that has most to lose from a damaged reputation). Close with Varian's ultimatum game (p. 583): theory gives the proposer almost everything, but laboratory offers average about 45 cents and small offers are rejected. Ask, as M&R FFT 5.1 does, how a taste for fairness or vengeance changes the commitment logic.

Push harder: is efficient bargaining an assumption or a result? (The Rubinstein equilibrium is efficient under complete information.) Address misconceptions explicitly: bargaining power does not decide efficiency; first-mover advantage vanishes as δ → 1; Coase says nothing about fairness, and rights still move wealth. End with the question for episode 2: if both parties can see the gains, why do they sometimes fail to trade at all?
