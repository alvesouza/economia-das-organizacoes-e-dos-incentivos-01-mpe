Talk through, in detail (3/3), the three hardest exercises of the Lecture 2 real-exercise list: a three-player mixed equilibrium and two short proofs. Solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 02.pdf` (items 8, 9 and 10), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 02 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture02 - Static Games of Complete Information II.pdf` (slides 14–17 and 44–51), the Tadelis book (Ch. 6) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§8.B and §8.D). The listener has heard Parts 1 and 2 and has the statements in hand. For the proofs, explain the idea before the steps, and say exactly where each hypothesis is used.

Item 8, Tadelis 6.8 (three-firm entry). An entrant earns 150 over the number of entrants minus 62: 88 alone, 13 with one rival, minus 12 with two; staying out pays 0. Classify profiles by the number of entrants. With none or one, an outsider gains by entering; with three, each loses 12 and leaves; with two, each entrant keeps 13 and the outsider would lose 12. The pure equilibria are the three profiles with exactly two entrants. For the symmetric mixed equilibrium, let each enter with probability p. A firm's payoff from entering depends on how many of the other two enter: 88 times (1 minus p) squared, plus 13 times 2p(1 minus p), minus 12 times p squared, which simplifies to 88 minus 150p plus 50p squared. Setting it equal to zero gives roots 4/5 and 11/5; only 4/5 is a probability. Check at 0.8: 3.52 plus 4.16 minus 7.68 is zero. Interpret: the market fits two firms, all three enter with probability 0.512, and expected industry profit is zero, all rents lost to miscoordination. Mention the asymmetric mixed equilibria: one firm enters for sure and the other two enter with probability 13/25. Traps: using a binomial over three firms instead of the other two; keeping 11/5, or discarding 4/5 because the payoff is zero (zero is exactly the indifference with staying out); claiming the symmetric equilibrium is the only mixed one.

Item 9, MWG 8.D.2: if exactly one profile survives iterated deletion of strictly dominated strategies in a finite game, it is a Nash equilibrium. Give the chain argument of slide 17. Take any strategy other than the survivor's. It was deleted at some round by a dominator that was still present then, and the survivor's opponents' profile was also present, since it is never deleted. So the dominator, or a pure strategy in its support, does strictly better against that profile. Repeat from the dominator: payoffs against the survivor's opponents strictly rise along the chain, so no strategy repeats, and finiteness forces the chain to end at a never-deleted strategy, the survivor's own. Hence the survivor is a strict Nash equilibrium, and the only one. Traps: proving the converse (every Nash equilibrium survives) and stopping, which shows only that the survivor is the sole candidate; assuming the dominator survives to the end; comparing payoffs against every opponent profile instead of the survivor's.

Item 10, MWG 8.D.6: every two-by-two game has a mixed-strategy equilibrium. Let p and q be the probabilities of each player's first strategy. Player 1's gain from his first strategy is affine in q; player 2's gain from hers is affine in p. Best responses are then steps: first strategy when the gain is positive, second when negative, anything when zero. The sign lemma: an affine function on zero to one either keeps one sign or changes sign exactly once. Case A or B, player 1's gain keeps a sign: he has a pure strategy that is always a best reply; pair it with player 2's best reply to it. Case C, it changes sign: player 1 is indifferent at q-star equal to (y minus l) over ((u minus w) plus (y minus l)), using the solution's labels; then check player 2's gain the same way, and if it changes sign too, both are indifferent at (p-star, q-star), a strictly mixed equilibrium. The step graphs must cross because indifference fills the gap that leaves Matching Pennies without a pure equilibrium. Note that the MWG manual's "all other cases" formula breaks when a player is indifferent to everything (zero over zero); the case split by the sign of the gain has no gap. Traps: proving only the generic case and dividing by a possible zero; forgetting that a pure equilibrium counts as mixed; citing Nash's theorem instead of giving the direct argument the exercise asks for.

Close by linking items 9 and 10 to the slides' two big results: Nash equilibrium and iterated dominance agree whenever the latter is decisive, and mixing guarantees existence (slide 51).
