Talk through, in detail (3/3), the hardest exercises of the Lecture 4 real-exercise list: hold-up, legal design and when moving first pays. Solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 04.pdf` (items 8, 9 and 10), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 04 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture04 - Credible Threats and Subgame Perfection.pdf`, and the books `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Ch. 8) and `Livro_Andreu Mas-Colell, Michael D. Whinston, Jerry R. Green - Microeconomic Theory (1995, Oxford University Press, USA) - libgen.li.pdf` (§9.B). The listener has heard Parts 1 and 2 and has the statements in hand.

Anchor: the Brexit lessons (slide 11) are talk is cheap, sunk investments can be strategic, and observability matters. Each item below adds one twist: who is exposed by a sunk cost, which institution makes a threat credible, and when being observed hurts.

Item 8, Tadelis 8.13 (Junk Mail Advertising). The buyer pays a commuting cost k, then the seller names a take-it-or-leave-it price. Once at the store, k is sunk, so the buyer accepts iff p is at most v. In an SPE he must accept at p equal to v; otherwise the seller would have no best offer. The seller asks v, so the buyer, anticipating it, stays home: payoffs (0, c), and the surplus v minus c minus k is lost. That is hold-up. In part (c), for any price strictly between c and v minus k, "I come and accept only up to that price" is a Nash equilibrium better for both, but walking out after a higher offer is not credible. In part (d), a postcard committing the seller to v minus k brings the buyer in, and the seller nets v minus k minus epsilon, more than c. The commitment works because it comes from the seller and is observed before the trip. Traps: letting the buyer reject at v; putting k in the acceptance decision; pricing the postcard at v.

Item 9, Tadelis 8.7 (Debt and Repayment). The lender may lend 100 to a debtor whose project returns 110; the debtor then repays any x. With no courts the debtor repays 0, so the lender does not lend: (0, 0). With free courts the lender sues after any repayment below 105, so the debtor repays 105, the lender lends, and both get 5. In part (c) the only pure Nash equilibrium is the SPE, because the lender controls his own suit plan and can always switch to lending. When the lender must pay a court fee of 105, suing costs more than it recovers, so the threat collapses and so does lending. Note the tie at x equal to 0, which must be broken towards not suing. Part (e): lending with a threat to sue is Nash and better for both, but not credible. With "loser pays", suing is credible again, the outcome is (5, 5), the lender would pay up to 5 for the reform, and the planner gains 10 of surplus. The fee is never paid, because nobody defaults on the path. Traps: thinking the lender's own off-path plan keeps him out in (c); leaving the tie at zero unresolved; counting the 105 fee as a cost of the reform.

Item 10, MWG 9.B.5 (Maskin). Simultaneously each player has m_i strategies. When player 1 moves first and is observed, player 2 has m_2 to the power m_1, one reply for each move. Part (c): without ties, player 2's reply to each move is unique and so is player 1's best move, so multiple SPNE require a tie. Part (d): if player 2 has no ties, player 1 can play his part of any pure Nash equilibrium of the simultaneous game and get at least that payoff in every SPNE. The bound can fail in a Nash equilibrium of the sequential game: in the solution's example, a non-credible threat lowers player 1 from 2 to 1. Part (e) gives two counterexamples. With an indifferent follower, tie-breaking can punish the leader, giving 0 against 1. In matching pennies, being observed destroys the value of being unpredictable: 0 against one half. Traps: giving player 2 m_1 times m_2 strategies; forgetting that the argument needs a unique best reply; claiming that moving first always helps.

Close by tying the three together: a sunk cost exposes whoever pays it, an institution can make a threat credible at no cost on the path, and moving first helps only when the follower's reply is pinned down and the leader has nothing to hide.
