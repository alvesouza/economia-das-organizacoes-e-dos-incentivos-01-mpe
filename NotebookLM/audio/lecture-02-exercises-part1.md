Talk through, in detail (1/3), the two-by-two matrix exercises of the Lecture 2 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 02.pdf` (items 1 to 4), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 02 - solutions.pdf` (the worked solutions and their "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture02 - Static Games of Complete Information II.pdf` (slides 9–17 and 42–51) and the Tadelis (Ch. 5–6), Gibbons (§1.3) and Muñoz-Garcia & Toro-Gonzalez (Ch. 2–3) books. The listener has the statements in hand. Before each solution, say what is asked and which tool answers it; after it, list the traps, why each is wrong and a quick test that catches it.

Start from the slides' tools. A best response is the best action against a given action of the other player (slide 10); a Nash equilibrium is a profile where every player best-responds to the others (slide 14), found by underlining best-reply payoffs cell by cell. For mixed strategies, the principle of indifference (slides 44–46): a player mixes only if the other's mix makes him indifferent, so each player's probabilities come from the other player's payoffs.

Item 1, Tadelis 5.6 (Hawk-Dove). Build the matrix from the story: two hawks split v and each loses k, so v over 2 minus k; two doves split v and each pays d, so v over 2 minus d; a hawk meeting a dove takes v and the dove gets 0. At v = 10, k = 6, d = 4 the cells are minus 1 each, 10 and 0, 0 and 10, and 1 each. Against a hawk the best reply is dove (0 beats minus 1); against a dove it is hawk (10 beats 1). The pure equilibria are (H, D) and (D, H), and this holds for every admissible parameter, because k exceeds v over 2. The symmetric mixed equilibrium has a hawk share of (v over 2 plus d) over (k plus d), 9/10 here. Traps: giving the dove a negative payoff against a hawk; writing v minus k instead of v over 2 minus k; reporting only (H, D) when the game is symmetric.

Item 2, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 2 Ex. 2 (Battle of the Sexes). Each player's best reply is to go where the other goes, so the pure equilibria are (F, F) with payoffs (3, 1) and (O, O) with (1, 3). Add the mixed one: the husband's indifference, 3q = 1 minus q, pins down the wife's probability of football, q = 1/4; the wife's, p = 3 minus 3p, gives the husband's, p = 3/4. Each expects 3/4, less than in either pure equilibrium, and they miscoordinate with probability 5/8. Traps: swapping the mixes; describing an equilibrium as one player's choice instead of a profile; calling the mixed equilibrium better because it is "fair".

Item 3, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 3 Ex. 1 (Chicken). No strategy is dominated: swerve is better against straight, straight against swerve. The pure equilibria are (Straight, Swerve) and (Swerve, Straight). Mixed: player 1's payoffs are 3 minus 3q from straight and 2 minus q from swerve, equal at q = 1/2; by symmetry p = 1/2, each expecting 1.5, and they crash with probability 1/4. Draw the best-response graphs: they cross at (1, 0), (0, 1) and (1/2, 1/2). Point out the book's printed line "3 + 3q = q + 2 − 2q": copied, it gives q = −1/4, which is not a probability; the correct line is 3 − 3q = q + 2 − 2q. Traps: solving player 1's indifference for p, when it contains only q; copying the typo; plotting player 1's best response with the axes swapped.

Item 4, Gibbons 1.12. Underline best replies and show the cycle: there is no pure equilibrium. Then argue that both players must mix: if one played pure, the other's reply would be pure and the cycle would continue. Player 1 is indifferent when 2q = 3 minus 2q, so player 2 plays L with probability 3/4; player 2 is indifferent when 2 minus p = 2p, so player 1 plays T with probability 2/3. Payoffs 3/2 and 4/3, and the best-response graphs cross only there, so the equilibrium is unique. Show the comparative static: raising player 1's payoff at (B, R) from 3 to 4 leaves player 1's own mix unchanged and moves player 2's to 4/5. Traps: solving the wrong player's probability; reporting (3/4, 2/3) without saying whose is whose; stopping at "no pure equilibrium" although Nash's theorem (slide 51) guarantees a mixed one.

Close with the rule all four share: underline cell by cell for pure equilibria, then write each player's indifference condition and read off the other player's probability.
