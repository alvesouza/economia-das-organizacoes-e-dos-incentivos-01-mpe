Talk through, in detail (2/3), the infinite-horizon cooperation exercises of the Lecture 5 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 05.pdf` (items 2, 3 and 5), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 05 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture05 - Repeated Games.pdf` (frames 18–32), `Aula_Lecture06 - Bargaining and Bayesian Games.pdf` (frames 5–8 on tit-for-tat) and the books `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§10.3) and `Livro_Felix Munoz-Garcia, Daniel Toro-Gonzalez - Strategy and Game Theory Practice Exercises with Answers, 2nd ed. (2019, Springer).pdf` (Ch. 6). The listener has heard Part 1 and has the statements in hand.

Set up the method once, from the slides. By the one-stage deviation principle (frames 24–25), a profile is subgame perfect iff no player gains from changing his action once, at one history, and then following the strategy. So there are always two checks: the cooperation phase and the punishment phase. Under grim trigger the cooperation check becomes the threshold delta-star equal to (v-d minus v-c) over (v-d minus v-star): the one-period gain from deviating divided by the gain plus the per-period loss once punished. In the slides' prisoner's dilemma, with payoffs 4, 5, minus 1 and 1, it gives one quarter (frame 29).

Item 2, Tadelis 10.2. B is strictly dominant for player 1 and R for player 2, so the stage equilibrium (B, R) pays (0, 0), which is also each player's minimax. For (M, C) the best deviation against C is B, worth 5: 4 over (1 minus delta) is at least 5 iff delta is at least one fifth. For (T, L) the best deviation against L is B, worth 8, not M, worth 7: 6 over (1 minus delta) is at least 8 iff delta is at least one quarter. The punishment phase passes because (B, R) is a stage equilibrium. Because the punishment is the minimax, no other strategies can lower these thresholds. Why the answers differ: from (M, C) to (T, L) the gain from cheating doubles, from 1 to 2, while the loss once punished rises only from 4 to 6. The better outcome is harder to sustain. Traps: using M as the deviation from (T, L), which gives a wrong one seventh; skipping the punishment-phase check; reading "better for both" as "easier to sustain".

Item 3, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 6 Ex. 1, punishments of fixed length. The stage payoffs are (C, C) worth 2, (NC, NC) worth 3, and 6 for confessing against a silent player. The gain from deviating is 3 and each punishment period costs only 1. The streams coincide after the punishment ends, so the condition is that the sum of delta to the j, for j from 1 to k, is at least 3. With one period, delta would have to be at least 3: impossible. With two, delta squared plus delta minus 3 must be non-negative, whose root is about 1.30: impossible. With ten periods the threshold is about 0.7627; grim trigger needs three quarters. Note that the book rounds the ten-period threshold down to 0.76, where cooperation actually fails. Traps: calling these strategies tit-for-tat (true tit-for-tat copies the last move and, as Lecture 6 frame 7 shows, is not an SPE for any delta in the slides' game); comparing the streams only up to the next period; rounding a threshold down.

Item 5, Tadelis 10.3 (Not-So-Grim Trigger), in the slides' game. The gain from deviating is 1 and each punished period costs 3, so the condition is 3 times the sum of delta to the j, for j from 1 to T, at least 1. With T equal to 1, delta-one is one third. With T equal to 2, the book's hint gives the cubic 3 delta cubed minus 4 delta plus 1, which factors as (delta minus 1) times (3 delta squared plus 3 delta minus 1). The threshold is the root of the quadratic: root 21 minus 3, over 6, about 0.264. Compare one third, 0.264 and the grim-trigger quarter: longer punishments need less patience, with diminishing returns because later periods are discounted more. Traps: forgetting that cooperation resumes (writing the deviation stream as grim trigger); keeping the trivial root delta equal to 1; saying a shorter punishment makes cooperation easier.

Close with the trade-off: a forgiving punishment is cheaper when a "deviation" was a mistake, but a weaker threat needs more patience.
