Talk through, in detail (2/3), the commitment exercises in quantity games of the Lecture 4 real-exercise list. Solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 04.pdf` (items 4, 5 and 7), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 04 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture04 - Credible Threats and Subgame Perfection.pdf`, and the books `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§8.2–8.3), `Livro_Felix Munoz-Garcia, Daniel Toro-Gonzalez - Strategy and Game Theory Practice Exercises with Answers, 2nd ed. (2019, Springer).pdf` (Ch. 4) and `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§2.2.A). The listener has heard Part 1 and has the statements in hand.

Anchor in the Brexit lesson (slide 11): a sunk, observable move changes what the other side will do afterwards. With continuous quantities the recipe is the same: solve the later stage for every possible earlier move, substitute, then optimize the earlier move. A strategy of a later mover is a whole function of what it observed.

Item 4, Tadelis 8.6 (Investment in the Future). Demand is 100 minus q. Firm 1 may pay F to cut its cost from 10 to 5 before Cournot. Solve both Cournot subgames, using the fact that each profit equals the firm's quantity squared. Without investment: 30 each, 900 each. With investment: firm 1 makes 100 over 3 and firm 2 makes 85 over 3, so firm 1 earns 10000 over 9. The threshold is F-star equal to 10000 over 9 minus 900, which is 1900 over 9, about 211. Show the decomposition: holding firm 2 at 30, the direct gain is only 156.25; firm 2's retreat adds about 54.9. About a quarter of the investment's value comes from its effect on the rival. For part (b), when F is above F-star but at most 10000 over 9, there is a Nash equilibrium in which firm 1 invests because firm 2 threatens to flood the market with 90 if it does not. The threat is not credible: after no investment, firm 2's best reply to zero is 45. Traps: computing F-star with firm 2 held at 30; writing strategies without the off-path quantities; offering a "non-SPE equilibrium" in which someone is not best-replying on the path.

Item 5, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 4 Ex. 11 (Stackelberg with two firms). Demand is 1 minus Q and the leader's cost is at most the follower's. The follower's best response is (1 minus c_F minus q_L) over 2, truncated at zero. Substitute it into the leader's profit to get q_L equal to (1 plus c_F minus 2c_L) over 2, q_F equal to (1 minus 3c_F plus 2c_L) over 4, and the price (1 plus c_F plus 2c_L) over 4. Both produce iff c_L exceeds (3c_F minus 1) over 2. Below that, the leader either sets the limit output 1 minus c_F or is a monopolist if entry is blockaded. With equal costs, total output is half of 1 minus c under monopoly, two thirds under Cournot, three quarters under Stackelberg and all of it under Bertrand, with prices in reverse order. The leader earns (1 minus c) squared over 8, more than its Cournot ninth; the follower earns a sixteenth. Traps: reporting the SPE as two numbers instead of the follower's whole function; solving both first-order conditions together, which gives Cournot; using the interior formula when it is negative.

Item 7, Tadelis 8.4 (Industry Leader). Firm 1 chooses q_1, then firms 2 and 3 choose simultaneously. Every value of q_1 starts its own proper subgame, a continuum of them, and the game has imperfect information because the followers move simultaneously. Each subgame is a Cournot duopoly on residual demand, with a unique equilibrium of (a minus c minus q_1) over 3 each. The leader then maximizes (a minus c minus q_1) over 3 times q_1 and chooses (a minus c) over 2. On the path each follower makes (a minus c) over 6, the price is (a plus 5c) over 6, and the leader earns (a minus c) squared over 12 against a Cournot sixteenth. For part (d), the followers could threaten to flood with a minus c unless the leader picks (a minus c) over 4: that is Nash, giving three-firm Cournot, but not subgame perfect, because each follower's best reply to a flood is zero. Traps: counting two subgames, one per stage; giving the followers' quantities instead of their reply functions; treating the followers as a Stackelberg chain.

Close with the common message: commitment pays through the rival's reaction, and only a reaction that is a best reply in its own subgame counts.
