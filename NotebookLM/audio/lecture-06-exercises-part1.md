Talk through, in detail (1/3), the bargaining exercises of the Lecture 6 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 06.pdf` (the statements; items 2, 3, 6 and 10), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 06 - solutions.pdf` (the worked solutions and their "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture06 - Bargaining and Bayesian Games.pdf` (frames 13–21) and the Tadelis book (Ch. 11). The listener has the statements in hand. Before each solution, say what the question really asks and which tool answers it. After it, list the traps, the reason each one is wrong, and a quick test that catches it.

Start from the slides' benchmark: in the ultimatum game the responder accepts every offer, including zero, and the proposer keeps everything. Explain why acceptance at indifference is forced in an SPE: if the responder rejected zero, the proposer would want the largest share below 1, which does not exist. In two-period alternating offers player 1 offers player 2 exactly delta_2, what she gets by waiting, and patience is bargaining power.

Item 2, Tadelis 11.1 (Disagreement). Build a Nash equilibrium with no agreement: player 1 demands the whole pie and player 2 rejects every offer. Check both deviations. Player 1 gets zero whatever he offers, so he cannot gain. Player 2 never receives a positive offer on the path, so rejecting costs her nothing. Extend it to any number of periods by making every proposer demand everything and every responder reject everything. Then say why this is not subgame perfect: in the last period, rejecting a positive offer is not a best reply. Traps: calling it an SPE because nobody deviates on the path; letting the proposer offer something positive, which makes rejection a mistake even in Nash terms; writing player 2's strategy as one action instead of a rule for every possible offer.

Item 3, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 4 Ex. 1 (ultimatum with envy and guilt). The responder's utility from accepting m is m plus alpha times (m minus (1 minus m)), that is m(1 + 2 alpha) minus alpha. Rejection gives zero, with no inequality term, because both get zero. He accepts iff m is at least alpha over (1 + 2 alpha), and the proposer offers exactly that. Read the money split at alpha one half (three quarters, one quarter) and at alpha 1 (two thirds, one third). As alpha grows the offer rises towards one half but never reaches it. Traps: writing x minus y as m minus one half instead of 2m minus 1; adding an inequality term to the rejection payoff; reporting the responder's utility, which is zero at the optimum, as if it were his money; claiming an equal split for a finite alpha.

Item 6, Tadelis 11.2 (Holdup). Solve backward. Whatever pie exists, player 1 takes all of it, so player 2's investment cost is sunk and earns her nothing; she chooses L. The SPE payoffs are (v_L, minus c_L), and this is not Pareto optimal because H creates more net surplus. Then part (b): player 2 invests H and threatens to reject any share below y; player 1 offers y. Both gain iff y lies between c_H minus c_L and v_H minus v_L, an interval that is not empty by the exercise's assumption. This is a Nash equilibrium but not an SPE: the threat is not credible once the cost is sunk. Link it to the Brexit lesson that sunk investments can be strategic, and to why contracts or vertical integration fix hold-up. Traps: "she invests H because the pie is bigger"; letting the sunk cost enter the acceptance decision; picking y outside the interval; calling (b) an SPE.

Item 10, Tadelis 11.7 (Baron–Ferejohn, the slides' homework 2). With T equal to 1 the proposer needs (N minus 1) over 2 other votes. Responders get zero by rejecting, so they accept zero and the proposer keeps the whole pie; each legislator's expected value before recognition is 1 over N. With T equal to 2, a responder in period 1 compares her share with delta times 1 over N. The proposer buys the cheapest (N minus 1) over 2 votes at delta over N each and keeps 1 minus delta (N minus 1) over 2N. Compare with two-person bargaining over two periods, where the first proposer gets only 1 minus delta: there the responder is sure to propose next, while here she is recognized with probability 1 over N and the proposer needs only half the votes. The share equals the infinite-horizon formula because the continuation value is 1 over N at every horizon. Flag the voting convention: with five or more legislators no single vote changes the outcome, so "everyone votes no" survives plain subgame perfection. Uniqueness needs legislators to vote as if pivotal. Traps: pricing a vote at 1 over N or at delta instead of delta over N; buying (N plus 1) over 2 other votes, which double-counts the proposer's own vote; reasoning "if I reject, I propose next".

Close with the common thread: in every bargaining exercise, write what each responder gets by rejecting, discounted by her own factor, and offer exactly that.
