Talk through, in detail (1/3), the finite-horizon exercises of the Lecture 5 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 05.pdf` (items 1, 6, 7 and 9), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 05 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture05 - Repeated Games.pdf` (frames 5–16) and the books `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§9.4, §10.1), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (§2.3.A) and `Livro_Luis M. B. Cabral - Introduction to Industrial Organization, second edition (2017, MIT Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (§7.3). The listener has the statements in hand.

Start with the slides' anchor. In a finitely repeated game the last period is the stage game, so it must be a stage Nash equilibrium after every history. If that equilibrium is unique, play unravels back to period one (frames 5–9). If there are several, the last period can carry a carrot and a stick (frames 11–13).

Item 1, Cabral 7.13. B and R are strictly dominant, so (B, R), paying (4, 4), is the unique stage equilibrium. In period T every subgame is the stage game scaled and shifted by a constant, so (B, R) is played. Given that, the continuation from any period k does not depend on what is played at k, and period k is again the stage game plus a constant. By induction, (B, R) is played in every period after every history, for any T and any delta. Traps: stating only the outcome and not the strategy after every history; arguing that a large delta or a long horizon allows cooperation; reasoning forward from period one instead of backward from T.

Item 6, Tadelis 9.1, played twice. The stage equilibria are (M, C), worth 5 each (the carrot), and (B, R), worth 1 each (the stick). One future period can therefore take away at most 5 minus 1, which is 4. A first-period profile is supportable iff each player's best one-shot gain is at most 4 delta. With delta equal to 1 the supportable profiles are the four sequences of stage equilibria and five carrot-and-stick paths: (T, L), (T, C), (T, R), (M, L) or (B, L), then (M, C). Walk through (T, L): the path gives 10 plus 5, 15; deviating to B gives 13 plus 1, 14. (M, R) and (B, C) fail because a gain of 5 exceeds the maximum punishment of 4, however patient the players are. The smallest delta is the largest gain divided by 4: three quarters for (T, L), (T, C) and (M, L); one quarter for (T, R) and (B, L). Mention renegotiation: (B, R) is worse for both than (M, C), so after a deviation both would like to forgive. Traps: giving the outcome without the off-path play; using the smaller of the two players' gains instead of the larger; claiming (M, R) can be sustained with enough patience.

Item 7, Gibbons 2.11. The stage equilibria are (T, L), paying (3, 1), and (M, C), paying (1, 2). (B, R) is not one: against R, player 1 prefers T, 5 against 4. Yet (4, 4) can be reached in stage one. Play (B, R) first; in stage two play (T, L) if stage one was (B, R) and (M, C) otherwise. Player 1's path gives 4 plus 3, 7, against 5 plus 1, 6, for deviating to T. Player 2 is already best-responding at (B, R): her path gives 4 plus 1, 5, against 2 plus 2, 4, for deviating to L. With discounting the construction survives down to delta one half. Renegotiation is not a worry here, because player 2 prefers the "punishment" (M, C). Traps: answering "no, because (B, R) is not a stage equilibrium"; using a mixed or non-equilibrium profile in stage two; checking only player 2 and missing that player 1 is the one tempted.

Item 9, Tadelis 10.7, the bartender. Watering the drink (x equal to 0) is strictly dominant, so the customer does not buy and payoffs are (0, 0). On ten evenings the unique stage equilibrium unravels: x equal to 0 and no purchase every evening. With an infinite horizon and grim trigger, the bartender pours a full drink iff (p minus c) over (1 minus delta) is at least p, that is iff p is at least c over delta. The customer buys iff p is at most v. Each punishment is the player's minimax, so the range from c over delta to v is exact, and it is non-empty iff delta is at least c over v. Example: c equal to 1, v equal to 3, delta one half gives prices between 2 and 3. Interpret this as a quality-assuring price: honesty must earn a premium above cost. Traps: letting the customer react on the same day (he pays before tasting, so the deviation earns the full p); giving only one player's constraint; thinking ten evenings is "long enough" for reputation.

Close: with a finite horizon, everything depends on whether the last period offers more than one equilibrium to reward and punish with.
