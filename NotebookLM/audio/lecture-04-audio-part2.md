Explain and describe in detail (2/2) Lecture 4, "Credible threats and subgame perfection": subgames and subgame-perfect equilibrium with the slides' examples, then the definition and payoffs of repeated games. Use the uploaded sources `Aula_Lecture04 - Credible Threats and Subgame Perfection.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis §8.2, §9.1, §10.1), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.4.B, §2.3.A) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§9.B, §12.D) for the formal side. The listener has heard Part 1 (the Brexit case solved by backward induction). Keep every claim precise, and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

From the case to a definition: several of the Brexit equilibria rest on threats that would not be carried out. Explain the slides' diagnosis: Nash equilibrium requires optimal play only on the equilibrium path, while backward induction requires it at every node, reached or not. Backward induction needs perfect information, so a general tool is needed: the subgame.

Subgames: give the definition, a single node and all its successors, never cutting an information set, inheriting information sets and payoffs. Use the slides' Example 1: from A the game goes to B, C or D, and after B to E or F, which share one information set. There are four subgames, from A, B, C and D. E and F start none, since each would cut their information set; B does start one, because the set {E, F} lies entirely inside it.

Subgame-perfect equilibrium: a behavioral profile whose restriction to every proper subgame is a Nash equilibrium there. Explain why every subgame-perfect equilibrium is a Nash equilibrium, the whole game being a subgame, and why the converse fails: subgame perfection rules out non-credible threats. State the Fact: in finite games of perfect information, subgame-perfect equilibria are exactly the backward-induction solutions.

Back to the Brexit game: it has nine subgames, the whole game, one at each EU node, and one at each of the six UK nodes after an offer, counting on the tree where Nature is replaced by expected payoffs. Explain why only the backward-induction solution of the nine Nash equilibria is a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, and show where (DF, FF) fails: in the subgame after no preparation and an unfavorable offer, rejecting is not a best response.

The voluntary battle of the sexes, Example 2 (Tadelis §8.2): player 1 chooses N, ending the game at (1.5, 1.5), or Y, leading to a simultaneous battle of the sexes paying (2, 1) at (O, o), (1, 2) at (F, f) and zero otherwise. Count two subgames, and list player 1's four strategies, YO, YF, NO and NF, explaining why NO and NF differ although N ends the game. Find the pure Nash equilibria, (YO, o), (NO, f) and (NF, f). Then replace and repeat: if the subgame plays (O, o), player 1 gets 2 and chooses Y; if (F, f), 1 is less than 1.5 and she chooses N; if the mixed equilibrium, Lecture 3's two thirds and one third, she expects 2/3 and chooses N. So there are three subgame-perfect equilibria, two pure, and (NO, f) is not one, since O is no best response to f inside the subgame. Draw the lesson: subgame perfection does not deliver uniqueness when a subgame has several equilibria, and the equilibrium expected there drives the earlier choice.

Repeated games: the same stage game played every period, with past actions observed. Give the slides' examples, firms in one market, legislators, teams, a firm and its suppliers, and pose the question: can the shadow of the future sustain what no one-shot equilibrium can, such as cooperation in the prisoner's dilemma?

The definition: the finitely repeated game G(T, delta) plays the stage game G in T consecutive periods, with discount factor delta in (0, 1], and player i's payoff is the sum over t from 1 to T of delta to the t minus 1 times u_i in period t. Read delta as patience, and explain why delta = 1 is allowed only because T is finite. Derive the value of a constant stream v, v times (1 minus delta to the T) over (1 minus delta). Explain the slides' three structural points: each period starts with the history observed, a strategy names an action for every period after every possible history, and each period starts a new subgame. Count strategies in the twice-repeated prisoner's dilemma: one first-period action plus one action for each of four histories, so 2 to the fifth, 32 pure strategies; a third period adds 16 histories. Subgame perfection must then hold after every history, on or off the path.
