Explain and describe in detail (2/2) Lecture 4, "Multistage games and bargaining": strategic bargaining with alternating offers. There are no slides for this lecture yet, so build the discussion on the uploaded sources `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis Ch. 11, pp. 220-237), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.1.D, pp. 68-71), the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (Appendix 9.AA, pp. 296-298, for the formal side) and `Livro_Paul Milgrom, John Roberts - Economics, Organization, and Management (1992, Prentice-Hall, Inc.) - libgen.li.pdf` (bargaining over a sale, pp. 140-142). The listener has heard Part 1 (multistage games, the carrot and the stick, the one-stage deviation principle).

The protocol: two players split a pie of size 1; player 1 proposes in odd rounds, player 2 in even rounds; x is always player 1's share; each rejection discounts the pie by delta; a hard deadline at round T gives both zero. Contrast with a repeated game: the game can end in any round and payoffs arrive only at agreement.

The ultimatum game: prove Prop. 11.1, that any split is a Nash equilibrium because the responder's rejection rule is never tested on the path. Then Prop. 11.2: the unique SPE gives the proposer everything. Explain carefully why the responder accepts at indifference: if she rejected x = 1, the proposer would have no best response, since any x below 1 is beaten by one halfway to 1. Discuss the laboratory evidence (offers of 25-50 per cent, rejections below about 20-25 per cent) and argue that it changes the payoff specification, not the logic of subgame perfection.

Finite horizon: solve two rounds aloud, giving (1 - delta, delta). Then run the backward-induction ladder for odd T and derive x_1 = (1 + delta^T)/(1 + delta), and for any T the single formula x_1 = (1 - (-delta)^T)/(1 + delta). Explain the zig-zag with the parity of T as a last-mover advantage that decays geometrically, faster when players are impatient, and the limit 1/(1 + delta) as a first-mover advantage. Prove Prop. 11.3, immediate agreement, from the fact that delay wastes pie. Then stress the order of limits: T to infinity first and then delta to 1 gives the equal split, while delta to 1 first at odd T leaves player 1 the whole pie.

Rubinstein's infinite horizon: there is no last round, but the subgame after two rejections is the original game. Present the Shaked-Sutton argument: the highest and lowest SPE payoffs of the first proposer satisfy v_low = 1 - delta v_high and v_high = 1 - delta v_low, so they coincide at 1/(1 + delta). Add Gibbons's fixed-point version, f(s) = 1 - delta + delta^2 s, a contraction with slope delta^2 below 1, and MWG's bound-squeeze proof as the formal side. Verify the strategies by one-stage deviations. With different discount factors derive x* = (1 - delta_2)/(1 - delta_1 delta_2), sign both derivatives, and state the lesson: patience is bargaining power. Then let delta_i = exp(-r_i Delta) and Delta go to 0: the share tends to r_2/(r_1 + r_2), the generalized Nash bargaining solution, which gives Nash's axiomatic weights a strategic foundation in relative impatience. Mention MWG's warning that with a fixed cost per round instead of discounting the uniqueness result can fail.

Legislative bargaining: Baron and Ferejohn, N legislators, a random proposer, majority rule, symmetric stationary equilibria. Under the closed rule derive k = 1 - delta (N - 1)/(2N), with each partner paid delta/N; explain why the proposer's share falls with N and delta yet rises relative to a partner. Under the open rule with three legislators, guaranteed success gives 1/(1 + 2 delta), and the risky minimal coalition, 2/(4 + 2 delta - delta^2) in expectation, beats it when delta exceeds the square root of 3 minus 1; note that delay then occurs in equilibrium. Lesson: the protocol is power.

Misconceptions to correct aloud: the infinite game is not the limit of finite games by definition, the match is a result; the first-mover edge comes from discounting, not from the deadline; and a more patient responder lowers, not raises, the proposer's share.

Private information: close with Milgrom and Roberts's example. Seller values 0 or 2, buyer values 1 or 3, trade efficient 96 per cent of the time; truthful reporting requires p >= 1.75 for the low seller and p <= 1.25 for the high buyer, so no price rule makes both truthful and trade efficient. Explain why this breaks Prop. 11.3's immediate efficient agreement, say the general mechanism-design theory is out of this course's scope, and preview Lecture 8, where delay signals strength.
