Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Lecture 4, "Credible threats and subgame perfection": Boris's Brexit dilemma as a game, from its strategic form to backward induction. Use the uploaded sources `Aula_Lecture04 - Credible Threats and Subgame Perfection.pdf` (the slides, which carry all the case data), `Aula_Lecture03 - Mixed Strategies and Dynamic Games.pdf` (for backward induction), `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis §8.1), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.1.A) and `Livro_Luis M. B. Cabral - Introduction to Industrial Organization, second edition (2017, MIT Press) - libgen.li.pdf` (Cabral §7.2 on commitment). The listener is an economics master's student who has heard Lecture 3 (extensive form, backward induction, subgame perfection). Keep every claim precise, and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

The data: costs in billions of pounds, payoff equal to minus cost, UK then EU. Hard Brexit with the UK unprepared costs 35 and 18; hard Brexit with the UK prepared costs 27.5 and 18, where 27.5 is 35 minus the 12.5 that preparation mitigates plus its cost of 5. A favorable deal costs 19 and 13, a neutral one 23 and 9, an unfavorable one 27 and 5. Preparation costs 5, all or nothing, and is sunk: with a deal it adds 5 to the UK's cost. Parliament approves a deal with probability 0.8; if it rejects, or if the UK rejects the EU's offer, the outcome is a hard Brexit.

The tree: the UK prepares or not; the EU, seeing this, offers a favorable, neutral or unfavorable deal; the UK accepts or rejects; Nature decides approval. Replace Nature by expected payoffs, which assumes risk neutrality. Compute one branch aloud: prepared and favorable, the UK gets 0.8 times minus 24 plus 0.2 times minus 27.5, which is minus 24.7, and the EU 0.8 times minus 13 plus 0.2 times minus 18, which is minus 14. Then give the rest: prepared, the neutral and unfavorable deals are worth minus 27.9 and minus 31.1 to the UK; unprepared, the three deals are worth minus 22.2, minus 25.4 and minus 28.6, against minus 35 for rejection. For the EU, accepted deals are worth minus 14, minus 10.8 and minus 7.6, and a hard Brexit minus 18.

The strategic form: the UK's eight rows combine prepare or not with an acceptance rule, accept any deal at least as good as unfavorable, neutral or favorable, or reject everything. The EU's nine columns name the offer if the UK prepared and the offer if not. Flag that the rows are a reduced set, threshold rules on the chosen branch only, and justify it: behavior on the unchosen branch never changes payoffs.

Too many Nash equilibria: the slides find nine pure equilibria. Focus on (DF, FF): the UK does not prepare and announces it will accept only a favorable deal, and the EU offers one. Test the threat in column FU, where the EU offers the unfavorable deal to an unprepared UK: the UK gets minus 35 by rejecting and minus 28.6 by accepting, so it would accept. But in column FF, DF and DU pay the same, because the unfavorable deal is never offered. Lesson: the strategic form cannot see whether a threat is credible, since threats live off the path.

Backward induction, the UK's last move: compare accepting with rejecting. Unprepared, the UK accepts any deal, since even minus 28.6 beats minus 35. Prepared, it accepts only the favorable deal: minus 27.9 and minus 31.1 are worse than minus 27.5. Say that preparation is what makes the threat to reject credible, and note how thin the neutral margin is: before weighting, a neutral deal costs 28 against 27.5 for a prepared hard Brexit.

Backward induction, earlier moves: the EU, anticipating, offers the favorable deal to a prepared UK, minus 14 against minus 18, and the unfavorable deal to an unprepared one, since minus 7.6 beats minus 10.8 and minus 14. At the root the UK compares minus 24.7 with minus 28.6 and prepares. State the solution: the UK prepares, accepts only the favorable deal if prepared and any deal if not; the EU plays FU; payoffs are minus 24.7 and minus 14. Point out that the EU's equilibrium plan for an unprepared UK is the very offer that exposed the threat in DF.

The lessons: sunk investments can be strategic, since the 5 billion is "wasted" whenever there is a deal yet it is what wins the good deal, by improving the outside option. Observability matters: ask what happens if preparation were secret, so the EU's offer cannot depend on it. Talk is cheap: announcing a rejection without preparing is not credible, since the EU knows the UK would accept. Connect this to Cabral's argument that commitment must be irreversible and observed, and to Lecture 3's Stackelberg leader.
