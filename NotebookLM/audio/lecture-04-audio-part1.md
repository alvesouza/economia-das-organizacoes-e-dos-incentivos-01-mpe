Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Lecture 4, "Multistage games and bargaining": multistage games, the carrot and the stick, and two-stage applications. There are no slides for this lecture yet, so build the discussion on the uploaded sources `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis Ch. 9, pp. 175-189, the book the course follows) and `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.2, pp. 71-82). The listener is an economics master's student who knows normal-form games, Nash equilibrium, backward induction and subgame perfection from Lectures 1-3. Keep every claim precise and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

Why stages matter: start from Tadelis's two questions, whether forward-looking players treat a sequence of games as one grand game, and whether they must play a stage-game Nash equilibrium in every stage. Explain why the answers are yes and no, and state the chapter's core insight: when future play can be conditioned on past outcomes, the set of self-enforcing outcomes becomes richer.

Definition and payoffs: a multistage game is a finite sequence of simultaneous-move stage-games, same players, each outcome observed by all before the next stage. Total payoff is the discounted sum v_i = sum over t of delta^(t-1) v_it. Introduce the Prisoner-Revenge Game: a prisoner's dilemma (mum or fink, payoffs 4,4 / -1,5 / 5,-1 / 1,1) followed by a Revenge Game with two pure equilibria, both loners at 0,0 and a gang war at -3,-3, plus a mixed equilibrium with q = 1/2. Have the hosts derive that q.

Strategies as history-contingent plans: each player has one stage-1 action and one stage-2 action for each of the four stage-1 outcomes, so 2^5 = 32 strategies and 1024 profiles. Explain why off-path entries are not decoration: they carry the threats.

Propositions 9.1 to 9.3: prove aloud that stage equilibria played unconditionally form an SPE for any delta, because unconditional later play adds a constant to every earlier cell. Then Prop. 9.2, the last stage must be a stage equilibrium, and Prop. 9.3, unique stage equilibria give a unique SPE. Draw the corollary: a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma unravels to fink in every period.

The carrot and the stick: construct the strategies (M, L, G, G, G) and (m, l, g, g, g). Derive that mum is a best response iff 4 >= 5 - 3 delta, so delta >= 1/3, and that the lopsided path (F, m) needs -1 >= 1 - 3 delta, so delta >= 2/3. Generalize to delta >= g/(c - s), one-shot gain over the carrot-minus-stick gap, and state Tadelis's two requirements: two distinct stage-2 equilibria, and enough patience. Explain why the efficient path is easier to sustain than the lopsided one: the same stick, a smaller temptation.

The one-stage deviation principle: state Def. 9.1 and Thm. 9.1 and walk through the proof by peeling off the last profitable deviation. Show how it reduces the carrot-and-stick check to five one-line comparisons, with the deviator following her own strategy afterwards. Flag that the infinite-horizon version needs discounting and returns in Lecture 5.

Gibbons's two-stage method: solve stage 2 for every stage-1 outcome, substitute, solve stage 1. Contrast three applications. Bank runs: with D > r > D/2 and R > D, withdrawal dominates at date 2, but the reduced date-1 game has two equilibria, a run and no run, a coordination failure rather than a dominant-strategy trap. Tariffs: with inverse demand a - Q and cost c, derive h_i = (a - c + t_i)/3 and e_j = (a - c - 2t_i)/3, then show the optimal tariff (a - c)/3 is dominant, yet both governments are worse off than under free trade, a prisoner's dilemma that motivates trade treaties. Tournaments: the boss picks the prize spread, the workers' first-order condition gives (w_H - w_L) times the integral of f squared equal to g'(e), and with normal noise the efficient spread is 2 sigma sqrt(pi); stress that the first mover designs the second-stage game. Say the hidden-effort question behind tournaments is moral hazard, outside this course's scope.

Misconceptions to correct aloud: a threat to play an action that is not a stage-2 equilibrium is not credible, so it cannot sustain cooperation; adding a common constant to every cell changes no best response; and with a unique stage-2 equilibrium no amount of patience helps.

Organizational reading: connect the carrot and the stick to relational incentives inside firms, where a future with a good and a bad equilibrium disciplines behaviour without a contract. Close by previewing Part 2: bargaining is the same logic run backwards from a deadline.
