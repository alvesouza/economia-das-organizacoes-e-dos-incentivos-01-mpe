Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Lecture 3, "Mixed strategies and dynamic games": mixed strategies, the principle of indifference and the existence of equilibrium. Use the uploaded sources `Aula_Lecture03 - Mixed Strategies and Dynamic Games.pdf` (the slides, with their appendix on price competition with capacity constraints), `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis Ch. 6), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §1.3) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§8.D) for the formal side. The listener is an economics master's student who has heard Lectures 1 and 2 (dominance, Nash equilibrium, Cournot, Bertrand). Keep every claim precise, and reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

Why randomize: some games have no pure-strategy equilibrium, as in a penalty kick, where each player wants to be unpredictable. Bertrand with capacity constraints is the slides' case: use the slides' numbers, demand Q(p) = 100 minus p, capacity 60 per firm, marginal cost 5; the cheaper firm sells first and the other serves the residual demand 40 minus p. Walk through the four cases and their deviations: both at cost, raising the price to 6 earns 34 on the residual demand; both at 10, cutting to 9.50 sells the full 60 and earns 270 instead of 225; unequal prices with the cheaper one at 40 or above, the expensive firm sells nothing and should undercut; unequal prices below 40, the capacity-bound cheaper firm should raise its price slightly. Name the Edgeworth paradox: dropping Lecture 2's unlimited capacity does not move the pure equilibrium, it destroys it.

Mixed strategies: define a mixed strategy as a probability distribution over S_i, a pure strategy as a degenerate one, and build the slides' three expected-payoff formulas: of a profile, of a pure strategy against sigma_{-i}, of a mixed strategy as a weighted average of pure ones. Stress independent randomization, and that expected payoffs need cardinal utility, unlike Lecture 1's ordinal dominance.

The principle of indifference: state both conditions, every strategy in the support earns the same expected payoff and none outside earns more. Prove both directions as the slides do: sufficiency because any deviation's payoff is a weighted average of pure payoffs, each at most the equilibrium payoff; necessity by moving the probability of a worse support strategy onto a better one, the sigma-hat construction. Draw the consequence: a mixing player is indifferent, so her probabilities do nothing for her; they are set to make the opponent indifferent.

Battle of the sexes: Alex and Chris, (O,O) pays (2,1), (F,F) pays (1,2), mismatches zero. With Chris at O with probability q, Alex earns 2q from O and 1 minus q from F, so O is a best response iff q is at least one third; Chris's cutoff is p of two thirds. Draw both best responses in the unit square and find the three intersections, (F,F), (O,O) and (2/3, 1/3). Show that each probability is pinned down by the other player's payoffs, and explain why each expects only 2/3, less than in either pure equilibrium: they miscoordinate with probability 5/9.

Interpretations and existence: discuss the slides' three readings, beliefs, empirical frequencies, population shares. State Nash's theorem (1950): every finite normal-form game has a mixed-strategy equilibrium. Answer the slides' "why?": a pure profile is a degenerate mixed one. Sketch the proof's logic from MWG §8.D: a fixed point of the best-response correspondence by Kakutani, which needs nonempty, convex values and a closed graph on a compact convex set, with convexity coming from indifference.

The appendix, price dispersion: unit demand, reservation value 1, capacity between one half and one. Reason why a firm either serves the residual demand at price 1 or undercuts, and why no pure equilibrium exists. Derive the symmetric mixed equilibrium from indifference: (1 minus F(p)) times capacity times p, plus F(p) times residual times p, equals the payoff at price 1. Solve for F and the support's lower bound; read it as sales and promotions.

The mini case "Work or shirk?": an employee works or shirks while the manager monitors or not; wage 10, effort cost 2, output value 20, monitoring cost 1; a caught shirker loses the wage. Explain the method only, without stating the answers: check each of the four cells for a profitable deviation, then write each player's expected payoffs and set the indifference conditions, noting that the shirking probability must make the manager indifferent and the monitoring probability must make the employee indifferent. Leave it to the listener to work out how cheaper monitoring or a higher wage moves each probability, by asking whose payoffs contain h and w.
