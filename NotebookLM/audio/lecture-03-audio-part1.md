Explain and describe in detail (1/2) Lecture 3, "Dynamic games: backward induction and subgame perfection": describing a dynamic game and refining Nash equilibrium. No slides exist yet, so follow `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis Ch. 7 and §8.1–8.2, the book the course follows), with `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.1.A, §2.2.A, §2.4) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§7.C, §9.B) for the formal side. The listener is an economics master's student who knows normal-form games, dominance, Nash equilibrium and mixed strategies (Lectures 1–2). Reason aloud through each argument instead of listing results.

Why the normal form is not enough: open with Tadelis's sequential Battle of the Sexes, where Alex phones Chris from the venue. Show why one prediction replaces the simultaneous game's three, and why moving first is not always good (Matching Pennies).

The extensive form: go through Tadelis's seven elements and stress element 5, what a player knows when she moves. Define game trees (Def. 7.1) and explain why one predecessor per node makes every outcome a unique path. Define information sets (Def. 7.2) and argue why nodes in one set must offer the same actions. Insist that "sequential" means a later mover observes the earlier move, not that she moves later on the clock. Separate complete from perfect information (Def. 7.3): every simultaneous game has imperfect information, and Tadelis also counts any move of Nature as imperfect. Mention that MWG's Def. 7.C.1 counts only singleton sets, so check the convention. Use the card game for Nature.

Strategies as complete plans: explain Def. 7.4 and why a strategy names an action at every information set, even sets the plan itself makes unreachable. Derive the product rule, a player with k sets and m_1, ..., m_k actions has m_1 times ... times m_k strategies, and walk through player 2's four plans oo, of, fo, ff. Contrast mixed and behavioral strategies with the library-of-manuals image. Show how to condition a mixed strategy into a behavioral one, and why the reverse map is a one-parameter family (three independent equations, four unknowns). State Kuhn's equivalence under perfect recall. Name the absent-minded driver only as the case where it fails, and say it is not examinable.

Nash equilibria of a tree: build the two-by-four matrix of the sequential Battle of the Sexes. Explain why each payoff appears twice, and find the three pure equilibria (O,oo), (O,of) and (F,ff). Use Def. 7.8 to say which information sets are off the path. Dwell on (F,ff): player 1 stays at F only because of what player 2 plans at a node never reached, a plan she would drop there. Explain why Nash equilibrium cannot catch this: it tests best replies from the root, so an off-path action costs nothing. Add Tadelis's remark that one matrix can come from several trees, so the matrix has thrown away exactly what is needed. Do the same with Gibbons's game, NE (R,(R',L')) and (L,(R',R')), and his phrase "noncredible threats".

Sequential rationality and backward induction: state Def. 8.1 and the procedure from the last nodes up. State Zermelo's theorem (Prop. 8.1, MWG Prop. 9.B.1), what each hypothesis buys, and why "no ties" is sufficient but not necessary for uniqueness. Solve the sequential Battle of the Sexes and Gibbons's three-move game. Discuss Gibbons's caveat: the argument needs player 2 to keep believing in player 1's rationality after a move that should never happen. Then the Centipede game (Tadelis Fig. 8.8): every mover stops, play ends at (1,1) although (3,3) is available, and stretching the game changes nothing. Bring in the evidence, McKelvey and Palfrey's lab subjects against Palacios-Huerta and Volij's chess players, and what it says about common knowledge of rationality.

Subgame perfection: explain why backward induction stalls at the voluntary Battle of the Sexes, where player 2's choice needs a belief. Define a proper subgame (Def. 8.2) by two tests: a singleton root, and no information set cut. Explain why the card game's nodes fail the second test, and note Gibbons's reason, that the history must be common knowledge inside the subgame. State Selten's Def. 8.3, and say why every SPE is a Nash equilibrium, why SPE equals NE when the only subgame is the whole game, and Tadelis's Fact that under perfect information SPE is backward induction. Teach the method: solve the smallest subgames, replace them by equilibrium payoffs, repeat, and branch when a subgame has several equilibria (MWG Prop. 9.B.3). Work the voluntary game to its two SPE, (YO,o) and (NF,f), and explain why (NO,f) is Nash but not perfect.

Close with Gibbons's outcome versus equilibrium distinction and preview Part 2: credibility, commitment and Stackelberg.
