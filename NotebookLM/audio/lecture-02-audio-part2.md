Explain and describe in detail (2/2) Lecture 2, "Static games of complete information II": Bertrand competition, mixed strategies and the existence of equilibrium. Use the uploaded sources `Aula_Lecture02 - Static Games of Complete Information II.pdf` (the slides), `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis §5.2.4, §6.1, §6.2, §6.4), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §1.2.B, §1.3), the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (§8.D, Prop. 8.D.1) and `Livro_Felix Munoz-Garcia, Daniel Toro-Gonzalez - Strategy and Game Theory Practice Exercises with Answers, 2nd ed. (2019, Springer).pdf` (Ch. 3). The listener has heard Part 1 (best responses, Nash equilibrium, Cournot).

Bertrand duopoly: same homogeneous good, but firms choose prices; common marginal cost c; the cheaper firm serves all demand Q(p) = (a minus p) over b, ties split it. Derive the monopoly price p^m = (a plus c) over 2. Then build the best-response correspondence case by case, as the slides do: if the rival prices below cost, any higher price; at cost, any price at least as high; above cost but at most p^m, no best response at all, since any undercut can be improved by a slightly higher undercut; above p^m, the monopoly price. Stress the empty case: the supremum is never attained.

Show that (c, c) is a Nash equilibrium, and that pricing at c is weakly dominated, connecting to Part 1's caveat. Then prove uniqueness in the slides' two steps. Step 1: unequal prices fail, case by case: a firm pricing below cost would rather sell nothing; a firm at cost earning zero would rather raise its price; a firm undercut while the rival is above cost would undercut back. Step 2: equal prices above cost fail, since a slight undercut nearly doubles profit; equal prices below cost fail too. State the Bertrand paradox: two firms reproduce perfect competition. List its three assumptions: homogeneous products, simultaneous choices, unlimited capacity. Finish with the slides' "Cournot or Bertrand?": what is harder to adjust, capacity (cars, cement, steel) or price (software). Compare prices: c under Bertrand, (a plus 2c) over 3 under Cournot, (a plus c) over 2 under monopoly.

Mixed strategies: define a mixed strategy as a probability distribution over S_i, the set Sigma_i, and why a player randomizes: to avoid being predictable. Give the slides' expected-payoff formulas, stressing independence across players and the fact that the payoff of a mix is a weighted average of pure-strategy payoffs. Define a mixed Nash equilibrium.

The principle of indifference: state it as an if-and-only-if, as the slides and MWG Prop. 8.D.1 do. Every pure strategy in the support earns the equilibrium payoff; none outside earns more. Prove both directions aloud. Sufficiency: any mix is an average of pure payoffs that are all at most the equilibrium payoff. Necessity: if two strategies in the support paid differently, moving the weight from the worse to the better raises the payoff by that weight times the gap. Draw the practical lessons: to verify an equilibrium, check pure deviations only; to find one, guess the supports and write the indifference equations.

Battle of the Sexes in mixed strategies: let q be the probability that Chris goes to the opera. Alex's payoffs are 2q and 1 minus q, so Alex prefers the opera when q exceeds one third. With p for Alex, Chris's payoffs are p and 2 minus 2p, so the threshold is p = two thirds. Draw the two best-response correspondences in the unit square and find the three crossings: both at football, both at the opera, and the mixed profile p = two thirds, q = one third. Emphasize the slides' lesson: each probability is pinned down by the other player's payoffs. Generalize with Alex's opera payoff alpha and Chris's football payoff beta, and show that raising alpha changes Chris's mix, not Alex's. Each expects two thirds, less than in either pure equilibrium, because they miscoordinate with probability five ninths.

Interpretations and existence: discuss beliefs, empirical frequencies and population shares as readings of a mixed strategy. State Nash's theorem: every finite game has an equilibrium in mixed strategies. Explain why a pure profile is a mixed profile, why the theorem is sufficient but not necessary (Cournot), and how Bertrand's empty best responses show what goes wrong with discontinuous payoffs.
