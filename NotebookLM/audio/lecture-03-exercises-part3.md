Talk through, in detail (3/3), the exercises of the Lecture 3 real-exercise list that push backward induction furthest: a centipede game and two proofs. Solve each step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. The listener has heard Parts 1 and 2 and has the statements in hand.

The anchor, slide 30: in a finite game of perfect information, backward induction always finds an SPE, and it is unique if no player is indifferent between two terminal nodes. Slide 35: in such games the SPE are exactly the backward-induction profiles.

Item 6, Tadelis 7.4 (centipede). A pot of 6 grows by half at each of four moves: 6, 9, 13.5, 20.25, and 30.375 if player 2 shares at the end. A grabber takes two thirds and leaves one third. Players 1 and 2 alternate, so each has two information sets and four strategies: GG, GS, SG, SS. The terminal payoffs are (4, 2), (3, 6), (9, 4.5), (6.75, 13.5) and, if player 2 shares at move 4, (20.25, 10.125). Backward induction: player 2 grabs at move 4 (13.5 beats 10.125), player 1 grabs at move 3 (9 beats 6.75), player 2 at move 2 (6 beats 4.5), player 1 at move 1 (4 beats 3). SPE (GG, GG), payoffs (4, 2). The Nash equilibria: the four profiles in which both players grab at their first move, whatever they plan later. Work forward to show that every Nash equilibrium, mixed ones included, has player 1 grabbing at once: many equilibria, one outcome. Part (e): if a share at the last node splits the pot equally, each gets 243/16, about 15.19. Now player 2 shares at move 4 (15.19 beats 13.5), and sharing runs back to the start: SPE (SS, SS). But the immediate grab is still a Nash equilibrium, so two outcomes survive. The lesson: changing only the last node's payoff reverses play from the first move. Traps: "one Nash equilibrium" instead of one outcome; misreading the last-node rule in (d), where sharing gives player 2 only a third; in (e), calling sharing the only equilibrium outcome.

Item 9, MWG 9.B.4 (zero-sum games of perfect information). Prove that all SPNE pay the same. Track values, not strategies. Induct on the longest path from a node to the end. At a terminal node the value is player 1's payoff. At a decision node, every action played with positive probability in any SPNE must lead to a successor of maximal value if player 1 moves, minimal if player 2 moves (her payoff is minus player 1's). So the node's value is that max or min, the same for every SPNE. At the root all SPNE pay (V, minus V). Zero-sum is what makes ties harmless: if the mover is indifferent, so is the opponent. Contrast the entry and Brexit games, where a follower's tie-breaking changes the leader's payoff. Traps: proving the SPNE is unique (only payoffs are); citing the uniqueness clause of slide 30, which needs no ties; forgetting mixing at tied nodes.

Item 10, Gibbons 2.1 (Rotten Kid theorem). The child picks A, producing incomes I_C(A) and I_P(A); then the altruistic parent picks a bequest B, of any sign, to maximize V(I_P minus B) plus k U(I_C plus B). Step 1: choosing B is choosing the child's consumption x, so the parent maximizes V(Y minus x) plus k U(x), where Y is family income. The optimal x depends only on Y, through V prime of (Y minus x) equals k U prime of x. Step 2: x rises strictly with Y; if it did not, the two first-order conditions would contradict each other. Step 3: the child's payoff is U of x of Y(A), strictly increasing in Y, so the selfish child maximizes I_C plus I_P. The parent's last move, which is sequentially rational, disciplines the child. Each assumption matters: the parent moves last, and B is unrestricted (a binding nonnegativity constraint breaks Step 1). Traps: letting the child maximize I_C alone; treating the bequest as a fixed number instead of a reply to the incomes; skipping strict monotonicity, which makes "maximize U of x" the same as "maximize Y".

Close: backward induction is about what each player will actually do when the moment comes, and that is what pins down behavior at the start.
