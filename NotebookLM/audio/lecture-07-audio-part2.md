Four auctions, one Bayesian game: lay out English, Dutch, first-price and second-price as a two-by-two of open versus sealed and pay-the-second-price versus pay-your-own. Define independent private values, and why the seller is not a player once the rules are set.

Second-price and English: prove Tadelis Prop. 13.1 aloud by the three regions of the highest rival bid, below the bid, between bid and value, above the value. Stress that your bid decides whether you win, never what you pay. Explain what weak dominance buys: no beliefs, so the common prior can go, robustness to correlated private values, and efficiency. Note it does not give uniqueness. Then the button auction (Prop. 13.2): quit when the price reaches your value, the same outcome as the second-price auction, and eBay's proxy bidding as that rule. Add Cabral's case for auctions: two buyers both worth 100 or both 150 give 125 by auction against 100 at a posted price, price discrimination by self-selection.

First-price and Dutch: explain why bidding one's value is dominated (Claim 13.1), and the trade-off between margin and win probability. Derive the symmetric BNE step by step, as in Tadelis eqs. 13.1 to 13.7: the expected payoff F of the inverse bid to the n minus 1 times the margin, the first-order condition, imposing the equilibrium, spotting the product rule, integrating by parts. Read the result as the expected highest rival value given it is below yours. For uniform values reach (n minus 1) over n times theta, and check Gibbons's two-bidder v over 2 both by the linear guess and by the differential equation. Use a value of 0.8 to show the payoff peaking at a bid of 0.4, and explain why shading falls as n grows. Explain why the Dutch auction is strategically equivalent to the first-price, while English and second-price coincide only in outcomes, and only under private values.

Revenue equivalence: state Tadelis's four conditions (well-behaved distributions, risk neutrality, highest type wins, lowest type earns zero) and what fails when each breaks, such as reserve prices or risk-averse bidders. Derive the order statistics: the maximum's c.d.f. F to the n, and the second highest's n F to the n minus 1 minus (n minus 1) F to the n. Compute expected revenue (n minus 1) over (n plus 1) both ways. Then show equivalence type by type: each bidder's expected payment is (n minus 1) over n times theta to the n in both formats, and his surplus theta to the n over n. Stress same mean, different risk: at n = 2 the price variances are 1/72 and 1/18, so a risk-averse seller prefers the first-price format. With Cabral, say that the format is then no revenue lever, so the choice rests on speed, transparency or resistance to collusion. Keep it to the uniform case; the general proof belongs to mechanism design, outside the course.

Common values: the swing voter's curse in Tadelis's jury, prior guilt q and signal accuracy p. Show a lone juror follows his signal only if p exceeds q, and that a pivotal juror with an innocent signal, conditioning on the other's guilty one, is left at the prior q and should convict, so voting one's signal is not an equilibrium. Then the winner's curse: Tadelis's oil field worth 10, 20 or 30, signals low or high, conditional values 15 and 25, and why bidding 15 with a low signal earns minus 1.25: it wins only when the rival also saw low, where the value is 10. Explain why English and second-price auctions stop coinciding here, since quitting prices reveal signals. Add Cabral's spectrum and offshore-lease evidence on the less informed bidder shading more.

Close by uniting lemons, the jury and the auction: in each, condition on the event in which your choice matters (acceptance, being pivotal, winning), the step that Lecture 8's signaling builds on.
