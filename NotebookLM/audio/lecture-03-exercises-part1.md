Talk through, in detail (1/3), the exercises of the Lecture 3 real-exercise list on trees, information sets and strategies: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 03.pdf` (items 1, 3, 7 and 8), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 03 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture03 - Mixed Strategies and Dynamic Games.pdf` (slides 20–28) and `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Ch. 7). The listener has the statements in hand. Describe every tree in words before solving it.

The anchor, from slide 25: a pure strategy is a complete plan, one action at every information set, including the ones the plan itself never reaches. Nodes in the same information set share one choice, because the mover cannot tell them apart.

Item 1, Tadelis 7.1 (counting strategies). With K information sets and m actions at each, a player has m to the power K pure strategies; with m_k actions at set k, the product of the m_k. The count is a product because a plan picks one action at every set independently. Traps: adding instead of multiplying, which counts actions, not plans; leaving out sets the plan makes unreachable; counting nodes instead of information sets.

Item 3, Tadelis 7.2. Player 1 chooses A, ending the game at (2, 0), or B. After B, player 2 chooses C, ending at (3, 1), or D. After D, player 1 chooses E, giving (0, 0), or F, giving (1, 2). Every node is its own information set: perfect information, 4 terminal nodes, 3 information sets. Player 1 has four strategies, AE, AF, BE, BF, because a plan starting with A must still say what he would do at his second node; player 2 has two. The normal form gives three pure Nash equilibria: (AE, D), (AF, D) and (BE, C). Backward induction picks one: at the last node F (1 beats 0); player 2, expecting F, picks D (2 beats 1); player 1 then picks A (2 beats 1). So (AF, D), payoffs (2, 0), is the unique SPE and the appealing one. (BE, C) rests on a threat to play E, burning both payoffs, that player 1 would not carry out. Traps: giving player 1 only three strategies, A, BE and BF (that is the reduced normal form); calling (BE, C) best because player 1 earns more; counting terminal nodes as information sets.

Item 7, Tadelis 7.9 (the dean). Nature gives the dean evidence with probability one half. The dean sees it, then accuses or bounces; an accused student, who does not know whether there is evidence, confesses or denies. Payoffs (dean, student): bounce (0, 0); confess (2, minus 2); deny with evidence (4, minus 4); deny without evidence (minus 4, 4). The game is zero-sum. The student's two nodes form one information set. The dean has four strategies (action with evidence, action without), and each cell of the matrix is the one-half/one-half average. No pure equilibrium exists: the best replies cycle. "Bounce with evidence" strategies are strictly dominated, so the dean always accuses with evidence and mixes only on bluffing. Indifference: the dean is indifferent when the student confesses with probability 2/3; the student is indifferent when the dean plays "accuse always" with probability 1/3. Unique equilibrium: bluff with probability 1/3 without evidence; confess with probability 2/3; payoffs (4/3, minus 4/3). Why the dean must bluff: if accusing revealed evidence, the student would always confess, which makes accusing without evidence pay. Traps: giving the dean two strategies instead of four; drawing the student's nodes as separate sets; using one state's payoffs instead of the expectation.

Item 8, Tadelis 7.10 (mixed versus behavioral). Player 2 moves first, a or b. After a, player 1 chooses L or R; after b, W or E. Player 2 then moves again without seeing player 1's move: t or z after a, x or y after b. Player 1 has 4 pure strategies; player 2 has three information sets, so 2 to the power 3, 8. For (b) and (c), the product of the behavioral probabilities is a mixed strategy that gives the same distribution over terminal nodes against every opponent strategy. That is Kuhn's equivalence under perfect recall (slide 28). In (d), merging player 2's two bottom sets breaks perfect recall. Behavioral to mixed still holds, but mixed to behavioral fails: one half (a, t) plus one half (b, z) correlates the later move with the first, and no behavioral strategy can. Traps: giving player 2 only 4 strategies; checking equivalence against one opponent strategy only; claiming the product construction breaks in (d).

Close: write every strategy as a full plan before looking for equilibria, then test the plans at nodes off the path.
