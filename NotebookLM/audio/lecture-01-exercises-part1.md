Talk through, in detail (1/3), the dominance and elimination drills of the Lecture 1 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 01.pdf` (items 1, 2, 3 and 5), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 01 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture01 - Static Games of Complete Information I.pdf` (slides 23–29), the Tadelis book (§4.1–4.2) and the Muñoz-Garcia & Toro-Gonzalez and Gibbons books. The listener has the statements in hand. Reason aloud: read every comparison entry by entry.

Anchor from the slides: a strategy is strictly dominated if another strategy of the same player pays strictly more against every strategy of the opponents (slide 23). IESDS deletes all strictly dominated strategies at once, round after round, until nothing more goes (slides 28–29). Round 1 uses only each player's rationality; every later round adds one more layer of "I know that you know". Give the method: write each strategy as its vector of payoffs against the opponent's surviving strategies, and compare vectors component by component. A single tie means weak dominance at most, and IESDS never uses weak dominance.

Item 1, Tadelis 4.5. Round 1: U is beaten by M everywhere (6 against 8, 2 against 4, 8 against 9) and goes. M against D is 8 equals 8, 4 equals 4, 9 against 6: only weak, so D stays. Round 2: on rows M and D, column C gives (4, 6) and R gives (5, 7), so C goes. Round 3 deletes nothing. Four profiles survive, M or D against L or R. Best-response marking then picks the two pure Nash equilibria, (M, R) and (D, L). Traps: deleting D because M is "never worse"; deleting C in round 1, when against U it beats R, 6 against 2; reporting one profile instead of the surviving set.

Item 2, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 1 Ex. 13. No player has a strictly dominant strategy. Player 1's best row is D against L but M against R. Player 2's R beats L in two rows but ties at zero in row D, so it is only weakly dominant. The only strictly dominated strategy is U, beaten by D (minus 10 against minus 9, 0 against 1). After U goes, R against L on rows M and D is (2, 0) against (0, 0): a tie, so nothing more goes. Four profiles survive. Traps: deleting L because R is "at least as good"; calling D dominant because it beats U, when a dominant strategy must beat every other strategy and M beats D against R; answering with payoffs instead of strategy pairs.

Item 3, Gibbons 1.2. First read the scan: the top-left cell "2.0" is the pair (2, 0). Round 1: B is beaten by T (2 against 1, 1 against 0, 4 against 3). C is not yet dominated: in row B it gets 2 while R gets 0. Round 2: on rows T and M, C gives (1, 2) and R gives (2, 3), so C goes. Survivors: T or M against L or R. Of these four cells only (T, R), with payoffs (4, 2), and (M, L), with (3, 4), are Nash equilibria; (T, L) and (M, R) survive IESDS but each has a profitable deviation. Traps: deleting C in round 1; misreading "2.0"; calling every survivor an equilibrium, when survival is necessary but not sufficient.

Item 5, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 1 Ex. 12. This item separates rationality from common knowledge of rationality. Neither player has a dominant strategy, since each best reply changes with the opponent's choice. The only strictly dominated strategy is player 2's B, beaten by C (4 against 3, 5 against 2, 4 against 3). Player 1's F is only weakly dominated by B. Part (c), rationality alone: player 2 drops B, but player 1 still evaluates his rows against all three columns, so six profiles survive. Part (d), common knowledge: player 1 now knows B is gone and compares rows on F and C only. There C gives (2, 0) and B gives (5, 2), so C goes, leaving F or B against F or C. The unique pure Nash equilibrium, (B, C) with payoffs (2, 4), lies inside. Traps: deleting F under weak dominance; letting player 1 drop B's column in part (c), which already uses player 2's rationality; thinking "no dominant strategy" means "no dominated strategy".

Close with the common thread: compare full payoff vectors, never single cells; a tie anywhere blocks strict dominance; and each round may only use the opponent strategies that are still alive.
