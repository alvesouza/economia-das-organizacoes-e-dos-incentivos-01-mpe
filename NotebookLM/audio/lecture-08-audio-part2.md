Explain and describe in detail (2/2) Lecture 8, "Signaling and perfect Bayesian equilibrium": Spence's job-market signaling, limit pricing as a signal, and the intuitive criterion. The listener has heard Part 1 (beliefs, PBE, sequential equilibrium, the MBA game).

Spence's model: present Gibbons's setup. Nature draws ability, the worker chooses education, competing firms pay expected productivity given their belief, and education is unproductive. Explain single crossing, a higher marginal cost of education for the low type, as steeper indifference curves, and why without it no signal can work. Use M&R's numbers: productivity 50 or 20, 30% high types, cost per unit 10 for the high type and 20 for the low type. Derive aloud the two self-selection constraints and the separating interval from (50 minus 20)/20 = 1.5 to (50 minus 20)/10 = 3. Explain why the low type chooses zero education (MWG Lemmas 13.C.1–13.C.2), why the high type over-invests, and why M&R call the expense "a pure social waste". Then pooling: the pooled wage 29, the pessimistic off-path belief that Gibbons admits "may seem strange", and the pooling set from 0 to 0.45. Discuss who gains: the low type always loses from signaling; the high type gains only if the share of high types is below 1 minus c_H/c_L, here 0.5. Stress that the separating set does not depend on the prior at all (MWG Fig. 13.C.8). Briefly show how hybrid equilibria connect the two families, with the low type mixing so that he is indifferent: at education 1 the wage is 40, the belief 2/3 and the mixing probability about 0.214, and the family runs from the pooling bound 0.45 to the separating level 1.5.

Limit pricing (Tadelis §16.2): demand P = 5 minus q in two periods, incumbent cost 1 or 2, entrant cost 2 and entry cost 1/2. Show why the entrant wants in only against the high-cost type. Explain Claims 16.1–16.2 and why the low type's monopoly output 2 cannot separate: mimicking gives the high type 4.25 against 3.25. Derive both incentive constraints as quadratics, giving the separating set from (3 plus root 5)/2, about 2.62, to 2 plus root 1.75, about 3.32. The low-cost firm over-produces to be believed. Then pooling: the Bayesian Cournot continuation with quantities 19/12, 13/12 and 5/6, the entrant's profit 7/36 (the book prints 7/18), and the pooling set from 1.493 to 1.917. Make the hosts notice that pooling deters no entry at all. Discuss when the limit price falls below marginal cost.

The intuitive criterion: start with Tadelis's speech of the high type who deviates to an MBA ("no L type in his right mind would do what I am about to do"). State Def. 16.1 carefully, noting that condition 2 should read the sender's payoff v_1, not v_2. Run it as a four-step test: equilibrium payoffs, best payoff from the unused action under any reply, which types are equilibrium-dominated, and whether a remaining type gains for sure once believed. Apply it three times. First, the MBA pooling equilibrium fails (the low type gets at most 5 below 6, the high type gets 8 above 6). Second, limit-pricing pooling fails at an output of 3 (7 beats 4 plus (19/12) squared), and only the separating output 2.62 survives. Third, in Spence every pooling point is broken by a band of deviations, from 1.35 to 2.4 at pooling education 0.3, and only the least-cost separating level 1.5 survives. Work Gibbons's Beer and Quiche: why pooling on Quiche passes the dominance test (Requirement 5) but fails equilibrium dominance (Requirement 6). Explain why the criterion is forward induction, and why its winner need not be the sender's favourite: above the 0.5 share the high type prefers the pooling outcome the criterion removes. Mention, as graduate background only, MWG's hierarchy of dominance tests in Appendix 13.AA.

Close with the lecture's one idea: everything turns on where beliefs are free. Free off-path beliefs kill threats no belief can justify, sustain continua of pooling and separating equilibria, and are disciplined by consistency and the intuitive criterion, which usually leaves the least-cost separating equilibrium.
