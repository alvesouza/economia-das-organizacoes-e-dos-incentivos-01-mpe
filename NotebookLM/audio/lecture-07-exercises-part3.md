Talk through, in detail (3/3), the signaling exercises of the Lecture 7 real-exercise list: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 07.pdf` (items 2, 5, 8 and 10, with Figures 9.1 and 9.11), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 07 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture07 - Bayesian Games, Adverse Selection and Signaling.pdf` (frames 38–50) and the Tadelis (Ch. 16) and Muñoz-Garcia & Toro-Gonzalez (Ch. 9) books. The listener has the statements in hand.

Set up the method once, from the slides' Spence model. For each candidate profile: (1) write each type's message; (2) compute the receiver's posterior by Bayes' rule after every message sent on the path: one after each message in a separating profile, the prior after the common message in a pooling one; (3) find the receiver's best reply; (4) for a message no type sends, find which off-path beliefs make some reply optimal; (5) check that no type gains by switching messages. Recall frame 45: separation needs c_H at most w at most c_L, a signal worth it for the high type and too costly for the low one.

Item 2, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 9 Ex. 1. Player 2 plays U iff her belief that player 1 is the high type is at least one third. The separating profile in which only the high type plays R is a PBE: Bayes' rule gives belief 1 after R, so U, and neither type wants to switch. The pooling profile in which both types play L, with player 2 answering D after R, is a PBE iff the off-path belief after R is at most one third. The other two candidates fail because the low type deviates. Traps: using the prior one half after R in a separating profile, when Bayes gives 1; "computing" an off-path belief with Bayes' rule, which is zero over zero; reporting the pooling PBE without the belief that supports it; skipping the low type's check.

Item 5, Muñoz-Garcia Ch. 9 Ex. 2 (job-market signaling, Figure 9.11). Education costs 4 for the high type and 7 for the low type, and does not raise productivity. The firm hires as manager iff its belief in high productivity is at least 2/5. The separating profile in which only the high type is educated is a PBE: manager after education, cashier without. The reverse separation fails, and so does pooling on education, because the low type deviates whatever the firm believes. Pooling on no education is a PBE for any off-path belief: with the prior the firm already hires everyone as manager, so a degree could only cost money. Traps: using one half instead of 2/5 as the cutoff; checking only the high type in the education pool; killing the no-education pool with an off-path belief that does not matter; confusing primed and unprimed labels.

Item 8, Tadelis 16.4 (School Choice). Here education is productive, so first check the firm's dominant replies. After Brown Bear, the high-tech job beats low-tech for both types (6 against 4 for E, 4 against 3 for G). After Silly Tree, low-tech beats high-tech for both. Remember that in a low-tech job the firm earns half the productivity minus the wage. Given those replies, E prefers Brown Bear (4 against 2) and G prefers Silly Tree (0 against minus 2). With p equal to one half the BNE are (BS, HL) and (SS, LL). The second relies on low-tech after Brown Bear, which is not a best reply to any belief, so the unique PBE is the separating (BS, HL), for every p. Separation works because the high-tech premium, 4, lies between the cost differences, 2 for E and 6 for G. Traps: giving the firm the full productivity in a low-tech job; charging the learning cost to the firm; searching for a pessimistic off-path belief that cannot exist when a reply is dominant.

Item 10, Tadelis 16.8 (Beer or Quiche). A wimp has probability 0.1. The preferred breakfast is worth 1 and avoiding a duel is worth 2; player 2 duels iff her belief in a wimp is at least one half. There are two pooling PBE: on beer, with a duel after quiche supported by a belief in a wimp of at least one half; and on quiche, with a duel after beer under the same kind of belief. No separating profile works, because avoiding the duel, worth 2, outweighs taste, worth 1. Now the intuitive criterion, step by step. In the quiche pool the wimp gets 3, its best possible payoff, and beer can give it at most 2, so drop the wimp from beliefs after beer. Then player 2 believes a beer drinker is surly, does not duel, and the surly type gains 3 instead of 2 by deviating: the quiche pool fails. The beer pool survives. Traps: running the criterion backwards; using the receiver's payoffs in the domination test (Tadelis's Definition 16.1 misprints this); setting the off-path belief equal to the prior 0.1; expecting separation just because tastes differ.

Close with the summary of frame 49: separating equilibria need costs that differ across types; pooling equilibria live on off-path beliefs, and refinements decide which of those beliefs are reasonable.
