Talk through, in detail (2/3), the oligopoly exercises of the Lecture 2 real-exercise list, where strategies are quantities or prices on a continuum: solve each one step by step and stop at every point where students usually go wrong. Use the uploaded sources `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 02.pdf` (items 5, 6 and 7), `Lista_Real exercises Lecture 02 - solutions.pdf` (worked solutions and "Common mistakes"), `Aula_Lecture02 - Static Games of Complete Information II.pdf` (slides 20–40) and the Tadelis (§5.2) and Gibbons (§1.2) books. The listener has heard Part 1 and has the statements in hand. Reason aloud through every derivation.

Anchor in the slides. In Cournot each firm chooses a quantity; its best response comes from a first-order condition, and the equilibrium is where best responses cross (slides 20–24): with two firms each produces two thirds of the monopoly output. In Bertrand firms choose prices for a homogeneous good, whoever is cheaper takes the whole market, profit jumps when prices cross, and there is no first-order condition (slides 29–39).

Item 5, Tadelis 5.8 (n-firm Cournot). First the normal form: n players, quantities from zero up, profit equal to price times own output minus cost, with demand a minus total output. Flag the book's typo: total output is q_1 plus up to q_n, not q_i plus up to q_n. The best response is the maximum of zero and (a minus c minus the rivals' total) over 2. Show that no firm sits at zero in equilibrium, then rewrite the first-order condition as q_i = a minus c minus Q. The right side is the same for every firm, so the equilibrium is symmetric without assuming it. Summing gives Q = n(a minus c) over (n plus 1), each firm (a minus c) over (n plus 1), the price (a plus nc) over (n plus 1) and profit ((a minus c) over (n plus 1)) squared. As n grows the price tends to c: the markup (a minus c) over (n plus 1) shrinks because each firm counts the price drop only on its own small share (slide 27). Traps: substituting (n minus 1)q for the rivals' output before differentiating, as if a firm moved its rivals' output too; assuming symmetry instead of showing it; concluding that the price goes to a because each firm's output goes to zero, when total output rises to a minus c.

Item 6, Gibbons 1.6 (Cournot with different costs). The best response is the maximum of zero and (a minus c_i minus q_j) over 2. Solving the interior system gives q_1 = (a minus 2c_1 plus c_2) over 3 and q_2 = (a minus 2c_2 plus c_1) over 3, an equilibrium only when both are positive. With both costs below a over 2 that holds; the price is (a plus c_1 plus c_2) over 3, each profit equals its output squared, and the cheaper firm produces more by exactly c_2 minus c_1. Rule out the corners to show uniqueness. When 2c_2 exceeds a plus c_1, the interior formula for firm 2 is negative; firm 1 produces its monopoly output (a minus c_1) over 2 and firm 2's best reply is zero, because firm 1's monopoly price (a plus c_1) over 2 is below c_2. Traps: reporting a negative output; dropping the "maximum of zero" from the best response; checking only that firm 2 stays out without ruling out the other corner and the zero-zero profile.

Item 7, Gibbons 1.7 (homogeneous Bertrand). Prove two things. Existence: at (c, c) both earn zero; raising the price loses all sales, cutting it sells at a loss. Uniqueness: label the firms so that p_1 is at most p_2 and go through four cases. (i) A firm priced below cost loses money and gains by pricing above the rival. (ii) A firm at cost facing a higher price can raise its price to the midpoint and earn a positive profit. (iii) A firm priced out undercuts. (iv) At a common price above cost, undercutting to the midpoint earns half the market's profit plus a positive extra, more than half the market. The result is the Bertrand paradox (slide 39): two firms already price at marginal cost, unlike Cournot's markup of (a minus c) over 3. It rests on homogeneous goods, unlimited capacity and a continuous price grid. Traps: using a first-order condition; forgetting case (ii), which looks competitive but is not an equilibrium; undercutting "by epsilon" without showing the gain.

Close with the contrast that slide 40 draws: quantity competition keeps a markup that shrinks with the number of firms; price competition with identical goods removes it with two.
