The worry: to verify a subgame-perfect equilibrium, must one test every combination of deviations, finking at stage 2 and switching again at stage 4? Fixing the others' strategies, a player faces a one-person decision tree, and the principle says single changes are enough.

The finite principle: define a one-stage unimprovable strategy (Tadelis Def. 9.1), no information set where changing only the action there, and following the strategy elsewhere, raises the payoff from that set on. State Theorem 9.1: a one-stage unimprovable strategy is optimal. Walk through the proof: take a profitable multi-stage deviation with finitely many changes and look at the last one. From there on it is a one-stage deviation, so by unimprovability dropping it does not lower the payoff; peel the changes off from the end until a single profitable one-stage deviation remains, a contradiction. This is backward induction in disguise. Stress that the deviation is evaluated with the player's own strategy continuing afterward.

The infinite horizon: state Tadelis Prop. 10.3, a profile is subgame perfect iff no player gains from one deviation at one history. Explain why discounting is what makes it work, continuity at infinity: changes after period N move the payoff by at most delta to the N times the payoff range over (1 minus delta), so any profitable deviation has a profitable finite truncation. Note that without discounting a deviation could pay only by changing infinitely many periods.

Applying it: grim trigger has two kinds of history. After an all-cooperative history the check is Part 1's inequality. After any deviation, playing M once against F earns minus 1 and then 1 forever, worse than 1 forever for every delta, so the punishment is credible. Then Tadelis's alternating path: (F, m) and (M, f) alternate, with reversion to (F, f) after any deviation, averaging (5 minus delta) over (1 plus delta) and (5 delta minus 1) over (1 plus delta), (2.158, 1.842) at delta 0.9, tending to (2, 2). Run the three checks and show that the bad period binds: accepting minus 1 now for 5 next period beats deviating to 1 forever iff delta is at least one half. Give the general form, (S plus delta T) over (1 plus delta) at least P.

Bargaining, the ultimatum game: player 1 proposes a split of a pie of size 1, keeping x; player 2 accepts or both get zero. State Tadelis Prop. 11.1, every split is a Nash equilibrium, supported by player 2's threat to reject worse offers, and Prop. 11.2, the unique subgame-perfect equilibrium offers x = 1 and player 2 accepts every offer. Explain why the responder accepts when indifferent: otherwise the proposer has no best offer.

Finite alternating offers: payoffs shrink by delta per round. With two rounds, work out (1 minus delta, delta). For T rounds give Tadelis (11.1), player 1's share (1 minus (minus delta) to the T) over (1 plus delta), and Prop. 11.3, agreement in the first round. Show the order of limits: T to infinity first gives 1 over (1 plus delta), then delta to 1 gives one half; delta to 1 first, with T fixed, hands the whole pie to the last proposer.

Rubinstein: with no last round, derive the share from the bounds argument (11.4)-(11.5), the lowest and highest equilibrium payoffs satisfying low = 1 minus delta times high and high = 1 minus delta times low, so the share is 1 over (1 plus delta), the number Gibbons reaches by his fixed-point argument. Verify the stationary strategies, offer 1 over (1 plus delta) and accept any share of at least delta over (1 plus delta), with the one-stage deviation principle, closing the loop with the first half. With different discount factors the share is (1 minus delta_2) over (1 minus delta_1 times delta_2): patience is bargaining power. Explain why agreement is immediate and efficient, and why the first-mover advantage vanishes as offers become frequent.
