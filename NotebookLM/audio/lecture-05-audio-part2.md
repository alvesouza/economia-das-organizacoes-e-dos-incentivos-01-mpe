Explain and describe in detail (2/2) Lecture 5, "Repeated games I: cooperation": grim trigger, the critical discount factor and the folk theorem. The lecture has no slides yet, so build it from `Livro_Steven Tadelis - Game Theory An Introduction (2013, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Tadelis §10.3, pp. 196–200, the condition (10.5) on p. 202, and §10.6, pp. 209–214), `Livro_Robert Gibbons - Game Theory for Applied Economists (1992, Princeton University Press).pdf` (Gibbons §2.3.B and Appendix 2.3.B, pp. 88–102), the Cabral `Introduction to Industrial Organization` source (§7.3) and the MWG `Microeconomic Theory` source (Appendix A to Ch. 12) for the formal side. The listener has heard Part 1: finite repetition unravels, two stage equilibria give a carrot and a stick, and delta can be read as a continuation probability.

Subgame perfection in the infinite game: state Tadelis Def. 10.5, a Nash equilibrium after every history, and stress that off-path histories are subgames too, so threats must be credible. There are infinitely many subgames, which is why Prop. 10.3, the one-stage deviation principle, matters: a profile is an SPE iff no player gains from changing her action at a single history. Explain footnote 6: discounting makes payoffs continuous at infinity, so an infinite profitable deviation implies a finite one.

Grim trigger: present the strategy on p. 198 as a two-state machine, cooperate until any outcome other than (M,m), then fink forever. Do the two checks aloud. In the punishment state, deviating to mum loses 2 today and gains nothing, so the threat is credible for every delta, by Prop. 10.2. In the cooperation state compare 4 plus 4 delta over (1 minus delta) with 5 plus delta over (1 minus delta). Multiply by (1 minus delta), simplify to 4 delta at least 1, and conclude delta at least 1/4, inequality (10.3). Separate the direct effect, plus 1 today, from the indirect effect, minus 3 in every later period. Add Gibbons's recursion V = 4 + delta V. Discuss Tadelis's "bootstrap": the carrot is the cooperative equilibrium itself, circular but consistent because every subgame is the same game, so the infinite horizon manufactures the second equilibrium that the finite game lacked.

The critical discount factor: derive (10.5) for any game, delta at least (v_d minus v_c) over (v_d minus v_star), and with prisoner's dilemma labels T > R > P > S, delta star = (T minus R) over (T minus P), the one-time gain over the gain plus the per-period loss. Reason through the signs: a larger temptation raises the threshold, a better reward lowers it, a milder punishment P raises it, and the sucker payoff S drops out because no one is ever the sucker on the path. Compare Tadelis's and Gibbons's 1/4 with Cabral's 1/2, where the gain is the same but the punishment costs only 1 per period. Give the three readings: patience; an interest rate, since delta = 1 over (1 + r) turns the condition into r at most (R minus P) over (T minus R), 300% here; and the expected number of periods. With several players, each needs her own condition, so delta must exceed the largest threshold (Gibbons p. 101), always below 1. Name MWG's Nash reversion and its result that a path sustainable at delta stays sustainable at any higher delta, so the threshold is a cutoff, not a window.

Feasible payoffs: define convex combinations and the convex hull (Tadelis Defs. 10.6–10.7) and work out 0.6 times (4,4) plus 0.4 times (5,−1) = (4.4, 2). Explain why average payoffs of repeated pure profiles span the feasible set, and why (2,2) is feasible although no cell pays it: by alternating over time, or with Gibbons's public randomizing device.

The folk theorem: state Theorem 10.1 with its two conditions, feasible and above a stage Nash payoff for every player, and delta close to 1. Give Tadelis's four-step intuition and Gibbons's proof for a stationary target, delta at least the maximum over players of (d_i minus x_i) over (d_i minus e_i). Then the alternating path to (2,2), pp. 212–214: derive player 1's average (5 minus delta) over (1 plus delta) using two geometric series in delta squared, and run the three checks. The punishment state always holds; the good period holds for every delta, since the quadratic 3 delta squared minus 2 delta plus 1 has a negative discriminant; the bad period needs delta at least 1/2 and binds. Point out that the sucker payoff matters here, unlike grim trigger. Name minimax (reservation) payoffs, Fudenberg–Maskin and MWG's individually rational folk theorem as not examinable.

Close with Tadelis's "almost anything goes": fink forever and lopsided paths such as (4.6, 1) are equilibria too, so the folk theorem explains how cooperation can be sustained, not that it will be, and it opens a selection problem.
